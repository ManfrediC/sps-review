ISOLATED HYPERTROPHY OF THE TIBIALIS ANTERIOR MUSCLE IN THE STIFF LEG SYNDROME A 63-year-old woman with a medic al history of insulin- dependent diabetes mellitus and pernicious anemia presented with progressive continous stiffness and superim- posed painful spasms in the left leg for 3 years. Her symp- toms were often triggered by su dden auditory and tactile stimuli or by emotional stress. These symptoms resulted in walking difﬁculties and limitations in daily activities. Clinical examination showed abnormal posturing of the left ankle. It was slightly dorsiﬂexed, and active and passive ankle movements were severely limited due to associated pain. There was striking isolated hypertrophy of the left tibialis anterior muscle. There were no signs of truncal rigidity or lumbar hyperlordosis. Brain and spinal MRI were normal. EMG showed a typ- ical pattern of continuous low-frequency ﬁring of normal motor unit potentials indistinguishable from voluntary muscle contraction in the tibialis anterior muscle, without concomitant antagonist muscle activation, despite attempts at muscle relaxation by the patient. Sudden auditory and tactile stimuli could not provoke muscle spasms during EMG examination. EMG of the abdominal, paraspinal, and other limb muscles showed no abnormalities. Because stiff leg syndrome was suspect ed, antiglutamic acid decar- boxylase (GAD) antibodies were assayed and found to be highly increased (>2000 U/ml; normal <5 U/ml). Insulin and islet cell antibodies were not measured. MRI of the lower limbs conﬁrmed isolated hypertro- phy of the tibialis anterior muscle (Fig. 1). Cerebrospi- nal ﬂuid (CSF) analysis was normal, and there were no oligoclonal bands. Diazepam 5 mg twice daily and monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions led to signiﬁcant clinical improvement. A diagnosis of stiff leg syndrome was made. To the best of our knowledge, muscle hypertrophy has never been described in the stiff person or stiff leg syn- drome. Several cases of stiff person syndrome were reported with symptoms conﬁned to one (most often dis- tal) limb, usually the lower leg. 1,2 The spasms are sponta- neous, reﬂex-induced, or induced by voluntary movement, in the absence of truncal rigidity or exaggerated lumbar lordosis. In contrast to the typical stiff person syndrome, serum/CSF anti-GAD antibodies and oligoclonal bands in the CSF are most often absent in the stiff limb syndrome, making a deﬁnitive diagnosis sometimes difﬁcult. 3 Isolated tibialis anterior muscle hypertrophy in our patient seemed to be related to persistent muscle con- traction, as has been seen in other muscle disorders associated with involuntary muscle contraction (e.g., myotonic syndromes, rippling muscle syndrome, and dystonia).
