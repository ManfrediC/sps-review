Anaesthetic management of a patient with Stiff–person syndrome Stiff–person syndrome (SPS) is a rare disease characterised by muscle rigidity and episodic spasms in the trunk and extre-mities. We report the anaesthet- ic management of three operations in a SPS patient. A 40-year-old female with invasive thymoma was scheduled for thymecto- my. She had been diagnosed as SPS based on her symptoms of constant stiffness in the legs and trunk accompanied by episodic painful spasms. Auto-antibody against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) was detected in her serum. After induction with fentanyl 2 lg.kg )1, thi- opental 3 mg.kg )1 and vecuronium 8 mg, anaesthesia was maintained with isoﬂurane 0.4–1%, nitrous oxide, addi- tional fentanyl and vecuronium. Soon after the operation, she was fully awake and train-of-four ratio monitored in the ulnar nerve recovered to 100%. Six weeks later, the patient under- went an emergency appendectomy. Anaesthesia was induced and maintained with diazepam 5 mg, fentanyl 4 lg.kg –l, thiopental 3 mg.kg )1, vecuronium 13 mg in total, isoﬂurane 0.8–1.5%, and nitrous oxide. The following year, she was sched- uled for endoscopic nasal sinus surgery. Anaesthesia was induced with fentanyl 2 lg.kg )1, propofol 10 mg.kg )1.h)1, vecuronium 0.8 mg.kg )1 and main- tained with propofol 3–4 mg.kg )1.h)1, nitrous oxide and additional fentanyl. The patient regained 25% T 1 twitch 30 min after the initial dose of vecuro- nium. Additional doses of vecuronium 1.5 mg were administered at about 30-min intervals. In the three operations, haemodynamic parameters were rela- tively stable and the patient awakened soon after anaesthesia. Symptoms of muscle rigidity and spasms temporarily improved after each anaesthesia. Auto-antibody against GAD, the enzyme essential for GABA production, is detected in 90% of SPS patients [1] and autoimmune pathogenesis has been strongly suggested in this disease [2, 3]. Loss of GABA-ergic inhibitory regula- tion on spinal and supraspinal [4] motor neurones may possibly result in sponta- neous ﬁring of motor units, leading to muscle rigidity or episodic spasms. Although isoﬂurane and propofol exert theiranaestheticeffectsthroughenhance- ment of GABA-ergic inhibition, they did not seem to have altered anaesthetic effects in our SPS patient, suggesting that control of the sensory system is relatively preserved in this disease. Temporary improvement of muscle rigidity and spasms after each anaesthetic was possibly caused by residual effect of isoﬂurane and propofol on motor units. Concerning neuromuscular blocking drugs, 25% twitch recovery time for vecuronium was within normal range in our patient, indicating that the neuromuscular junc- tion is not affected in this disease. In summary, in contrast with a case report describing prolonged hypotonic- ity after general anaesthesia in SPS [5], the effects of general anaesthesia or neuromuscular blocking drug were not altered in our SPS patient.
