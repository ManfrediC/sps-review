StiffMan Syndrome E. ROSS CLIFTON, DO, and S. H. SUBRAMONY, MD, Jackson, Miss ABSTRACT: Stiff-man syndrome (SMS) is a rare disorder of the central nervous system characterized by painful involuntary stiffening of axial muscles accompanied by spasms. These are often precipitated by tactile or emotional stimuli, volitional movement, and startling noises. Until recently, little has been known about the pathogenesis of SMS. There is evidence now to support the hypothesis that SMS may be an autoimmune disease. This is leading to changes in the way this disease is diagnosed and treated. We describe two patients with SMS and review the pathogenesis, diagnosis, and treatment. STIFF-MAN SYNDROME (SMS) was originally described in 1956 by Moersch and Woltman,! who observed 14 patients over 32 years. These patients had involuntary stiffening of muscles, especially axial muscles, and painful muscle spasms. Only 84 cases of SMS have been reported.? In 1967, Gordon et al? reviewed 44 cases in the world liter- ature and proposed diagnostic criteria for SMS based on clinical and electromyographic findings as well as effects of neuromuscular blocking agents. Clinical findings’ included the following: (1) Symmetrical, progressive, continuous stiffness of axial musculature becomes constant in weeks; ““stone hard’’ and ‘“‘board-like’” are descriptions that were given to muscles. (2) Spasms are precipi- tated by noise or emotional stimuli. (3) Rigidity is abolished in sleepXthough others* have since reported that in a series of 13 patients, many had only a decrease in muscle activity during sleep). (4) As a rule, patients have normal motor and sen- sory function except for difficulty in ambulation and limitation of range of motion, which exacer- bates the condition. (5) Patients generally have normal intellect. Objective neurophysiologic tests such as elec- tromyography (EMG), response to myoneural blocking agents, effect of chemical block of peripheral motor fibers, and response to general anesthesia are also used to help establish the diag- nosis of SMS. Shortly before referral the paraspi- nal muscles became involved, and the neck muscles became so rigid that she could not move her head. The spasms were painful and were exacerbated by loud noises, emotional upset, and cool damp weather. She did not notice appreciable change during sleep. There was no history of stroke, she had had no surgery, was not diabetic, and had no thyroid dysfunction. Some years earlier, renal tubular acidosis (idiopathic type) had been diagnosed. She had no history of neurologic problems or autoimmune disease. She was taking Bicitra (sodium citrate and citric acid) and potassium supplements. Physical examination showed a pleasant woman in moder- ate distress. General physical examination was unremarka- ble except for neurologic findings. She was alert and oriented. Muscle tone was increased especially in the back and lower extremities. Strength could not be adequately assessed because of the rigidity. Reflexes were normal and plantar responses were flexor. No sensory abnormalities were identified. Laboratory studies showed normal values for electrolytes, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, liver panel, and ceruloplas- min. Creatine phosphokinase value and results of a thyroid profile were also normal. Serum protein electrophoresis showed a polyclonal gammopathy. Magnetic resonance imag- ing (MRI) of the brain and spine was unremarkable. The elec- troencephalogram (EEG) revealed no epileptiform activity. Electromyography showed continuous motor unit activity resembling normal recruitment that the patient could only par- tially suppress. Serum was obtained, and glutamic acid decar- boxylase (GAD) autoantibodies, assayed by Solimena et al® were present. Cerebrospinal fluid was not obtained. The patient was treated with baclofen (Lioresal), 120 mg per day in divided doses, and diazepam (Valium), 15 mg/day, and showed significant improvement over the next 8 months. She was able to discontinue the diazepam and decrease the baclofen to 10 mg/day. She has returned to work. Case 2. A 25-year-old black man was referred because of muscle stiffness and painful spasms, especially of the thighs, abdomen, and shoulders, which had insidiously developed in 1987. His motion was limited by these episodes. Gradually, the stiffness and spasms became painful and continuous and were exacerbated by noises or emotional upset. He believed the stiffness was diminished by sleep, but did not completely subside. Muscle biopsy in 1988 was unremarkable, and the disorder was thought to be ‘‘functional.’’ Eventually, the patient sought further medical attention because walking had become nearly impossible. His neck was becoming so rigid that he could not turn his head. He could not dress himself or care for his personal hygiene. There was no medical his- Clifton and Subramony © STIFF-MAN SYNDROME 711 tory of diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, or previous neuro- logic illnesses. There was no family history of neurologic or autoimmune disorder. The spasms were worse in cold weather, and whenever anyone touched him the spasms became more painful. Emotional upset also exacerbated the spasms. Physical examination was unremarkable. Neurologically, the patient was alert and oriented and appeared to be of nor- mal intellect. He had a pronounced lumbar lordosis. His prox- imal muscles were rock hard and somewhat hypertrophied. There was no significant weakness, and findings on sensory examination were normal. Reflexes were normal and plantar reflexes were flexor. His gait was severely limited by the stiff- ness of his axial muscles. He could not bend down to put his shoes on. He walked with a cane and had a tendency to lose his balance. Results of admission laboratory studies were nor- mal, except for an elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK) value of 729 U/L. Aldolase was normal. The EEG was unremarkable as was the chest x-ray film. Cranial MRI re- vealed a congenitally narrow corpus callosum and ischemic changes in the right centrum semiovale. Electromyography revealed abnormal involuntary continuous motor unit poten- tial firing, with normal appearance of individual motor unit potential. Cerebrospinal fluid studies showed normal glucose and protein values and no cells. A sample of CSF was sent to Solimena et al5 for assay; no autoantibodies to GAD were found. Tests for parietal (CSF) cell antibodies were also nega- tive. Thyroid profile yielded normal findings. Treatment was begun with baclofen, 20 mg three times per day. He has gradually become more self-sufficient, and although he still has significant stiffness, the spasms are less painful. DISCUSSION Pathogenesis and Etvology Several possible etiologic mechanisms for SMS have been proposed and ultimately rejected.*° Psychiatric disease, metabolic disorders,® and Renshaw cell dysfunction®!° are such etiologic mechanisms that have been explored and rejected. Guilleminault et al‘! and Schmidt et al!? postu- lated that SMS may be due to an imbalance between catecholaminergic and gamma- aminobutyric acid (GABA) (ie, GABA-ergic) sys- tems. Meinck et al® further supported this GABA- ergic catecholaminergic theory. They found that the beneficial action of diazepam could be explained as being either the result of inhibition of central catecholamine neurons or of activation of GABA-ergic mechanisms or of both. Miller and Korsvik!? and Whelan‘* reported improvement in two cases of SMS with administration of baclofen, an analog of GABA. Spehlmann et al! added sodium valproate to a treatment regimen in one case of SMS, with remarkable results. Because sodium valproate is thought to augment GABA- ergic transmission, more credence was given to the GABA-ergic-catecholaminergic theory. In 1980 Maida et al'® suggested that SMS may be of inflammatory origin, based on elevation of IgG and acute phase reactant levels in one patient. Werk et al’? described a patient with SMS and thyrotoxicosis. This relationship had been previ- ously investigated by Gordon et al,? who con- cluded that the two disease processes were not related, since the patient still had SMS after he was successfully treated for thyrotoxicosis. In 1988 Solimena et al'® described a patient with SMS, type I diabetes mellitus and epilepsy. The presence of autoantibodies and her HLA pheno- type led them to consider SMS as an autoimmune disease. They found autoantibodies to GABA- ergic neurons in their patient’s CSF and serum. Glutamic acid decarboxylase, the enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to GABA, was the principal autoantigen. Solimena et al> fur- ther investigated autoimmune relationships in 33 patients with SMS; in 20 of them, autoantibod- ies to GABA-ergic neurons were found to be the principal autoantigen. There was also a signifi- cant association between SMS and _insulin- dependent diabetes mellitus. They concluded that GAD autoantibodies are detectable in at least 60% of patients with SMS and that autoantibodies against GABA-ergic neurons are a useful marker in the diagnosis of the disease. They also suggested a possible clinical difference between patients with SMS who have antibodies to GABA-ergic neu- rons and those who do not. Treatment Until recently, benzodiazepines have been the mainstay of treatment of SMS.'° Other GABA- ergic drugs such as baclofen'* and sodium valproate’ have also proved effective in treatment of SMS. Plasmapheresis*® and corticosteroids? have been reported to be beneficial. CONCLUSIONS Both cases fulfill the clinical criteria proposed to suggest the diagnosis of SMS.** In agreement with Lorish and Thoroteinsson,* we did not believe that myoneural blockade, general anesthe- sia, or peripheral nerve block, as Gordon et al? proposed, would add to the diagnostic specificity. Gordon et al based this idea on the work of Werk et al,!” who showed that succinylcholine abolished electromyographic activity at rest and during startle-induced spasm. We also concur with Lor- ish and Thoroteinsson* that these maneuvers may needlessly add to the risk of patient morbidity. Both patients had episodes of aching and stiffen- ing of the axial musculature that progressed to involve the limb and neck muscles. Both patients had difficulty moving about, and both had pain- ful spasms, especially when startled or emotion- ally upset. Neither patient could detect significant difference in the stiffness during sleep. Both patients had normal intellect and normal findings 712 July 1992 ¢© SOUTHERN MEDICAL JOURNAL © Vol. 85, No. 7 on neurologic examination except for the limita- tions in movement due to the excessive stiffness. Both patients had similar electromyographic pat- terns demonstrating continuous motor unit poten- tials. Both responded favorably to baclofen, though the patient in Case 1 had a more signifi- cant response. Interestingly, the patient in Case 2 was thought to be “‘hysterical,’’? and others?!:?2 have reported similar labeling of their patients. Patient 1 had autoantibodies to GABA-ergic neu- rons and patient 2 did not. Neither patient had thyroid disease, seizures, or diabetes mellitus, and neither had a family history of autoimmune disease. Over the past 2 years, the diagnosis of SMS has been supplemented by the determination of anti- bodies against GABA-ergic neurons. There has also been increasing evidence that SMS may be of autoimmune etiology, primarily based on the work of Solimena et al.'® The two patients presented here have no currently identified autoimmune disease, and one of the two has no autoantibodies to GABA-ergic neurons; however, both fulfilled the clinical criteria for SMS and both had electromyographic findings supportive of the diagnosis. There were no significant clinical differ- ences between these two patients with regard to antibody status, as suggested by Solimena et al.° Our first patient had hyperchloremic acidosis consistent with renal tubular acidosis (RTA), which has been associated with a variety of autoimmune diseases, most commonly Sjégren’s syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and lupus anticoagulant.?3 Whether RTA itself is an autoimmune disease has not been determined, but it is known that there is a high prevalence of renal tubular acidosis associated with other autoimmune diseases. Therefore, more credibil- ity is given to the hypothesis that SMS is an autoimmune disease. It would also be useful to investigate the occurrence of antibodies to GAD in patients with Sjégren’s syndrome. Acknowledgment. We thank James J. Corbett, MD, for his review of the manuscript.
