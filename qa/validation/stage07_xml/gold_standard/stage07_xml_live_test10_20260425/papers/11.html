Movement Disorders Vol. 6, No. 1, 1991, pp. 73-75 0 1991 Movement Disorder Society Brief Report A Therapeutic Trial of Milacemide in Myoclonus and the Stiff-Person Syndrome P. Brown, P. D. Thompson, J. C. Rothwell, B. L. Day, and C. D. Marsden MRC Human Movement and Balance Unit, and University Department of Clinical Neurology, Institute of Neurology, London, England Summary: We investigated the therapeutic effects of milacemide in seven patients with myoclonus and three patients with the stiff-person syndrome in an open-label trial. Milacemide was initiated at 800 mg/day and was gradually increased to a maximum dosage of 2,400 mg/day. No significant improvement occurred in the 10 patients. Key Words: Myoclonus-Stiff-person syndrome-Milacemide. Glycine is an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS (1). It causes membrane hyperpolarisation and inhibits neuronal firing (2). Defective glycinergic systems may be important in animal models of spinal rigidity (3,4), and this has led to speculation that abnormalities of glyciner- gic inhibition by spinal interneurones may be important in the stiff-person syndrome (5). Several animal studies have also suggested that defec- tive glycinergic systems may result in myoclonus. Thus, the stereotactic infusion of the glycine receptor antago- nist, strychnine, into the medullary reticular formation induces generalised stimulus-sensitive myoclonus in the rat (6). In urea-induced myoclonus, urea has been shown to displace glycine at its receptor sites in the brainstem (7), and in DDT-induced myoclonus, intraventricular gly- cine is effective in suppressing the myoclonus (8). In Poll Hereford calves with inherited myoclonus, there is a spe- citic and marked deficit in strychnine binding sites in the spinal cord, reflecting a loss of, or defect in, glycine re- ceptors (9). Milacemide is a glycine precursor that penetrates the blood-brain barrier effectively, before being converted into glycinamide and glycine (10). It is reasonably well tolerated (1 1). We report an open-label trial of milacemide in seven patients with myoclonus of various types, and three patients with the stiff-person syndrome. PATIENTS AND METHODS Ten patients were enrolled in the study (Table 1). There were four patients (cases 14) with postanoxic multifocal action myoclonus. The action myoclonus was cortical in origin, and a cortical correlate in the backaveraged elec- troencephalographic activity preceded and was time- locked to the jerks occurring on action. Somatosensory evoked potentials were normal in these four patients, and there was no evidence of stimulus-sensitive myoclonus. One patient (case 5) had myoclonic dystonia. The jerks markedly improved with alcohol. He was the product of a prolonged and difficult labour. There was no relevant family history. There was no electrophysiological evi- dence that the myoclonus in this patient was cortical in origin. One patient (case 6) had propriospinal myoclonus of unknown aetiology, and has been previously reported (12). Another patient (case 7) had axial myoclonus and paroxysmal axial spasms, presumed to be propriospinal in origin, and has also been previously reported (P. Brown et al., unpublished observations). The final three patients (cases 8, 9, and 10) had slowly progressive axial stiffness, with electrophysiological evidence of continu- ous muscle fibre activity at rest in paraspinal muscles. All three patients had serological evidence of autoimmune disease, one patient had a history of pernicious anaemia, and a second had a history of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus. Two of the three patients had anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies in the cerebrospinal fluid. The diagnosis of stiff-person syndrome was made in these three cases. In the latter, a score of 0 indicated normal performance, and 3 severely impaired performance, needing total assistance. Clinical and func- tional ratings, and a videotape recording of the clinical assessment, were made at the beginning and end of the study. The clinical and functional rating scores on treatment were expressed as percentages of the pre- milacemide treatment scores. A change in score on treat- ment with milacemide of &20% was considered as indi- cating no improvement, 2140% as slight improvement, and >41% as marked improvement. Full blood count, electrolytes, liver function tests, and anticonvulsant lev- els were also monitored at the beginning and end of the study in each patient. Treatment present at the start of the trial was kept un- changed during the study. Milacemide was started at 400 mg b.d., and increased by a further 400 mg b.d. every 34 days, up to a maximum dosage of 1,200 mg b.d. The final assessment was made after 3-4 days with this dosage, except in case 7, in whom the final assessment was made after 3 days with 400 mg b.d. RESULTS The clinical rating score failed to change significantly in the 10 patients treated with milacemide. In one patient (case 4), there was a mild improvement in the functional assessment score, but in another patient (case 3) this score deteriorated. Milacemide was generally well tolerated, although one case (case 10) complained of drowsiness and constipa- tion, and a second patient, who had epilepsy (case 4), had a generalised seizure during treatment. Full blood count, electrolytes, liver function tests, and anticonvulsant lev- els did not change significantly on treatment. DISCUSSION Experimental evidence in animals implicating glyciner- gic dysfunction in spinal rigidity (3,4) has led to trials of glycine in the stiff-person syndrome (14,15). These trials were unsuccessful, at least partly because of the weak penetration of the blood-brain barrier by glycine (16). Milacemide penetrates the blood-brain barrier well, be- fore being converted into glycinamide and glycine (10). Despite this, milacemide, up to a dosage of 2,400 mg daily, was ineffective in the three patients with the stiff- person syndrome. Most pharmacological and immunolog- ical evidence points to an imbalance between catechol- aminergic and GABAergic systems in this condition (17). Similarly, animal models of myoclonus, implicating gly- cinergic dysfunction (69) in this condition, led Truong and Fahn (16) to investigate the therapeutic effects of glycine in patients with various types of myoclonus. They found no evidence for an antimyoclonic action for glycine in doses up to 6 g per day, and again attributed this to the poor penetration by glycine of the blood-brain barrier. Movement Disorders, Vol. 6, No. 1, 1991 MILACEMIDE IN MYOCLONUS 75 However, in the present study, milacemide also had no consistent effect in seven patients with myoclonus. The contrasting effects of milacemide treatment on the func- tional abilities of cases 3 and 4 were of a degree reported in spontaneous fluctuations by both patients. The ineffec- tiveness of milacemide suggests that defective glycinergic transmission may not be an important factor in postan- oxic cortical action myoclonus, myoclonic dystonia, or propriospinal myoclonus. Most studies of postanoxic my- oclonus have implicated abnormalities of serotonergic and GABAergic transmission (13,18). The apparent ineffectiveness of glycine (16) and mila- cemide in treating myoclonus in humans contrasts with the animal evidence that implicates defective glycinergic transmission in experimental myoclonus (6-49, and with the ability of milacemide to improve DDT-induced myoc- lonus (8). However, most of these animal experiments involve manipulations of the reticular formation of the lower brainstem, and as such, may be inappropriate mod- els for cortical and spinal myoclonus. The effectiveness of glycinergic drugs in brainstem reticular reflex myoclo- nus remains untested. Acknowledgment: We thank G. D. Searle Ltd., High Wycombe, England for supplying the milacemide.
