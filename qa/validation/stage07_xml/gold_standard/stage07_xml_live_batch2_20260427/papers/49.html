1027 Caution in use of interferon for male infertility SiR-Treatment of idiopathic male infertility is difficult and the number of new drugs is increasing. No workers have shown in double-blind trials improved sperm count or motility in placebo-treated controls. We were surprised by Yamamoto and Miyake’s (Aug 27, p 614) report of successful use of interferon alfa for male infertility. They gave this drug at the daily dose of 3 MU, 5 days per week to 3 patients with severe oligozoospermia and 1 with azoospermia, and recorded substantial improvements in sperm density and motility after two months’ treatment. This treatment was so successful that 2 patients’ wives became pregnant. Yamamoto and Miyake classified these patients as having idiopathic infertility, reporting in all cases normal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinising hormone, and testosterone plasma concentrations. The normal plasma FSH in these patients suggests that the spermatogenetic process proceeds at least to spermatid level in order to maintain the inhibitory feed-back on this gonadotropin. We have recorded spontaneous improvement of seminal indices in 8 men presenting with important idiopathic oligozoospermia and normal plasma FSH.’ Assessment of testicular cytology by fine-needle aspiration done at the time of diagnosis showed normal maturation germ line on both testes. It has been postulated that in these cases some injurious effects of, for example, drugs or systemic acute illness could have induced transient spermatogenic cells damage, and that cytological analysis may have identified the tubular improvement before semen count has been restored. If these factors applied to Yamamoto and Miyake’s patients the improvement was not attributable to interferon. Moreover, normal plasma FSH concentrations associated with azoospermia or oligozoospermia are also seen in patients showing testicular cytological pictures of complete or partial spermatid arrest.’,2 If such patients are given interferon, the drug could stimulate late maturative phases of spermatogenesis. This hypothesis is dangerous without evidence from experimental studies and double-blind therapeutical trials. A simple incidental observation does not justify use of this expensive drug in the treatment of idiopathic male infertility (50% of male infertility). Genetics of susceptibility and resistance to insulin-dependent diabetes in stiff-man syndrome SiR-Stiff-man syndrome (SMS) is a rare disorder of the central nervous system characterised by muscle rigidity and spasms. Autoantibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) are found in 60% of patients,’ suggesting an autoimmune pathogenesis. SMS is often associated with other autoimmune diseases including insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM). Since GAD is an autoantigen both in SMS and IDDM, the two diseases may correlate with different immune responses to GAD that are at least in part genetically determined. We have HLA typed 18 SMS patients’ and found that, as in IDDM, SMS was associated with the allele DQB1*0201. 1028 Other DQB1 alleles rarely found in IDDM (*0602 and sequence-related *06 alleles) were found in 10 SMS patients at the frequency observed in controls. 5 of 8 SMS patients lacking a *06 allele had diabetes by contrast with 2 of 10 patients with a *06 allele, suggesting that *06 might be associated with a reduced prevalence of diabetes in SMS. To test this hypothesis we followed up our SMS patients for the possible development of diabetes. Since our 1993 report3 additional SMS patients now have diabetes. All 3 patients had DQB1*0201 and 2 lacked *06 alleles. Diabetes has thus now developed in 7 of 8 SMS patients lacking a *06 allele and in 3 of 10 with a *06 allele (p=002). 10 of 13 SMS patients with 1*0201 have diabetes versus 3 of 10 of those with a *06 allele (p=003). Moreover, we have recently found that first-degree relatives of IDDM patients with *0602 and SMS-like GAD autoantibodies are at very low risk of diabetes.4 Perhaps an SMS-like-anti-GAD response is associated with different rates of progression to overt diabetes depending on the HLA genetic background. Diabetes may develop very slowly or not at all in individuals with a subset of anti-GAD autoantibodies and IDDM-protective DQB1 alleles. We suggest that DQB1*0602 (and other sequence-related *06 alleles) confers protection from diabetes in patients with SMS and relatives of patients with IDDM despite anti-GAD autoantibodies. By contrast, among SMS patients, 1*0201 is associated with high risk of diabetes, especially when protective *06 alleles are absent. Colitis associated with non-steroidal anti- inflammatory drugs SIR-The dangers of upper gastrointestinal side-effects of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are well known,’ but the capacity of these agents to injure colonic mucosa (ie, NSAID-associated colitis2) remains unrecognised by many gastroenterologists. A recent editorial on the aetiology of colitis made no specific reference. 3 Between March, 1991, and June, 1994, we saw 60 new cases of proctitis, proctocolitis, and colitis and in 23 (38%) colitis had developed while the patient was taking an NSAID. This came to light as a result of exhaustive drug histories. We included a thorough inquiry into analgesic usage by listing names of possible NSAID. We also asked about over-the-counter consumption. None of the 23 patients had pre-existing inflammatory bowel disease that could have been exacerbated by NSAID ingestion. All patients presented with diarrhoea, with or without blood loss per rectum, and all had negative stool cultures. The diagnosis was established by colonoscopy within 7 days of presentation. The use of the colonoscope rather than a rigid sigmoidoscope is of particular relevance since NSAID- associated colitis frequently spares the rectum. The mean age of patients with NSAID-associated colitis was 53, with a range of 23-79. A wide variety of NSAIDs were implicated but diclofenac (12 cases) and mefenamic acid (5 cases) were the commonest. The NSAID had usually been taken orally but colitis was seen after rectal and intramuscular administration and could occur within a few days of therapy. Endoscopic appearances varied from a mild patchy non- ulcerative colitis with areas of punctate erythema and mucosal oedema to a picture indistinguishable from fulminating ulcerative colitis. Histological findings were non- specific, though ulceration and crypt abscesses were frequent. Granuloma formation was not seen. NSAID-associated colitis was frequently mild and rapidly improved on withdrawal of the drug and a 4-8 week course of oral sulphasalazine or mesalazine (5-aminosalicylic acid). More severe cases received systemic and topical steroids, and 1 patient required colectomy for a fulminating colitis and toxic megacolon after intramuscular diclofenac. On the whole, however, a patient with NSAID colitis will have a very good chance of full recovery. So far there have been no spontaneous relapses among the 23 patients; 1 had a recurrence of colitis after inadvertent re-exposure to an NSAID. NSAID-associated colitis seems to be an under-recognised but common form of colonic disease. We suggest taking a thorough drug history in every new case of colitis. Once NSAID-associated colitis is recognised a reassuring prognosis can be given to the patient. Long-term prophylaxis with sulphasalazine or mesalazine seems unnecessary. The psychological benefits for patients of knowing they do not have a chronic disease with the likelihood of relapse and the possible long-term risk of cancer are obvious. The cost saving in the avoidance of regular follow-up and life-long drug taking could be considerable.
