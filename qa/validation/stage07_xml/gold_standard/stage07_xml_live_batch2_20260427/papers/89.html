Powers3** Glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) is an autoantigen in two autoimmune diseases, insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM) and stiff-man syndrome (SMS). However, most individuals with one of these diseases do not have the other disease. Prior studies have suggested that the natures of the GAD Abs associated with each of these diseases are different, which may have impli- cations for the autoimmune pathogenesis. We have compared the GAD autoantibody profile and have mapped GAD protein epitope regions in the two diseases using an immunoprecipitation assay with recombinant GAD 65 and GAD 67 proteins, GAD protein fragments, and synthetic GAD peptides, as well as chimeric GAD proteins. Our results indicate that individuals with SMS have GAD Abs in 100- to 500-fold higher titer than individuals with IDDM. The population of GAD Abs in SMS sera is quite complex and includes those that recognize at least three GAD 65 epitope regions located between amino acids I-16,188-442, and 442-563. These types of GAD Abs are not found in IDDM sera. All SMS sera also had Ab specificity that binds GAD 67 in a region highly homologous to amino acids 188-442 of GAD 65. In contrast to prior studies that used immunoblotting to measure GAD Abs, we find GAD Abs in SMS sera also target two conformation-dependent regions of GAD 65, one located in the middle and one near the C-terminus of the protein. These two regions of the GAD 65 protein are similar to regions targeted by GAD 65-specific Abs found in individuals with IDDM. These results indicate that although disease-specific epitopes may exist, there is also overlap in the humoral response between the two diseases. The journal of Immunology, 1996, 156: 818-825. Differences in the N-terminus of the protein are responsible for targeting GAD 65 and GAD 67 to different intracellular locations (7). In contrast to rat pancreatic islets that express both GAD 65 and GAD 67, human pancreatic islets contain only GAD 65 (8, 9). GAD autoantibodies were initially discovered in individuals with SMS (3). Subsequent studies by Baekkeskov et al. demon- strated that GAD autoantibodies are also found in individuals with IDDM (1 0). GAD autoantibodies are an important marker of the autoimmune process of IDDM since they are present in the vast majority of individuals with new-onset diabetes as well as in many individuals in the prediabetic stage of the disease (I 1-13). The possible role of GAD as the primary autoantigen in IDDM has been suggested by recent demonstrations of cellular autoimmunity directed at GAD in IDDM (14, 15) as well as autoimmunity di- rected at GAD in an animal model of IDDM (16, 17). Although GAD Abs are found in both SMS and IDDM, the GAD autoantibody profile in the two diseases differs. For example, GAD Abs in SMS bind denatured GAD protein utilized in immu- noblotting while the GAD autoantibodies in IDDM do not bind the protein under such conditions (IO). In addition, co-incubation of purified GAD protein and SMS sera inhibits the enzymatic activity of GAD, which suggests that these GAD Abs from SMS bind the active site of the enzyme (18). This inhibition does not occur with GAD Abs from IDDM. Other studies of the GAD Abs in the two diseases have focused on the GAD protein epitopes at which the autoantibodies are di- rected. Utilizing immunoblotting of recombinant GAD protein, Butler et al. mapped the major GAD 65 epitope in a large number of individuals with SMS to the COOH terminus of the protein (amino acids 475-484 and amino acids 571-585) (19). Kim et al. recently reported that SMS sera have higher GAD autoantibody levels than IDDM sera and target a con- tinuous protein epitope located in the first eight amino acids of GAD 65 (21). The N-terminal epitope was not bound by IDDM sera. These results suggest that SMS sera have GAD Abs that bind linear protein epitopes. Conversely, GAD Abs in IDDM do not bind denatured GAD protein, GAD fragments, or GAD peptides, which implies that these GAD Abs bind an epitope dependent on protein conforma- tion (10, 22, 23). This concept has led to the suggestion that GAD Abs in SMS and in IDDM differ in their epitope recognition pat- tern and that disease-specific epitopes may exist (1 8, 19, 21). How- ever, a limitation of prior reports is that studies with SMS sera were optimized for detecting Abs that bind linear epitopes (immu- noblotting), while studies in IDDM used immunoprecipitation of nondenatured GAD protein as the assay system. Because of these experimental differences, the results of epitope mapping are diffi- cult to compare. To compare the GAD Abs in the two diseases and to determine if disease-specific GAD epitopes exist, we have mapped the protein epitope regions in sera from individuals with SMS and individuals with IDDM in an immunoprecipitation assay using recombinant GAD 65 and GAD 67 proteins, GAD protein fragments, GAD peptides, and chimeric GAD proteins. Materials and Methods Preparation of GAD protein Full length rat GAD 65 and human GAD 67 cDNAs were transcribed into GAD RNA using T7 RNA Polymerase in an in vitro transcription system (Megascript, Ambion, Austin, TX) (23). Metabolically labeled, recombi- nant rat GAD 65 and human GAD 67 proteins were prepared by in vitro translation of GAD RNA with reticulocyte lysate (Promega, Madison,WI) using an amino acid mixture containing ["Slmethionine (1 200 Ci/mmol) (23). TCA precipitation of the translation products was utilized to deter- mine the percentage of incorporation of the labeled amino acid in the GAD protein. Percentage of incorporation was calculated as (radioactivity in TCA-precipitated proteidtotal radioactivity in the reaction) X 100 and ranged from 20 to 60%. The rat GAD 65 protein reacted with a widely used sheep polyclonal GAD antiserum and two mAbs, GAD 1 and GAD 6 (24, 25). The polyclonal GAD antiserum was a gift of Dr. Enrico Mugnaini (University of Connecticut, Hartford, CT) and Virginia Weise (Laboratory of Clinical Science at the National Institute of Mental Health, Bethesda, MD) and reacted with both GAD 65 and GAD 67. The GAD 1 mAb was a gift of Dr. David Gottlieb (Washington University, Washington, MO); the GAD 6 mAb was obtained from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank at the University of Iowa, Ames, 1A (National Institute of Child Development Contract No. I-HD-2-3144). As shown by other groups, the GAD 6 mAb reacted only with the GAD 65 isoform (9. 19, 21, 26, 27). GAD 65 protein fragments containing amino acids 1-195 and 1-360 were prepared from restriction enzyme fragments of GAD 65 cDNA after linearization with HinclI and NsiI enzymes, respectively. PCR was utilized to create GAD 65 cDNA fragments encoding amino acids 188-585 and 444-585 (23). Each primer used was complementary to published GAD cDNA sequence with incorporation of a unique restriction enzyme site to facilitate subcloning. The PCR products were either purified using a Gene- clean I1 kit (Bio 101, Vista, CA) after restriction enzyme digestion or directly subcloned into pT7 Blue plasmid (Novagen, Madison, WI). The GAD 65 cDNA fragment encoding amino acids 188-585 was digested with Stul to create a cDNA fragment encoding amino acids 188-442. A GAD 65 cDNA fragment encoding amino acids 361-585 was created with a unique BglII site in the GAD 65 cDNA. Fragments of the GAD 65 protein were then prepared from the cDNA fragments by in vitro transcription and translation as described above. Since there are 30 pmol of hot ["Slmethi- onine and 175 pmol of unlabeled methionine in the translation reaction, approximately one in six to seven methionines will be labeled (28). Since incorporation of [3sSJmethionine will be a random event. the number of methionine residues in a protein fragment was linearly related to the amount of label incorporated (R2 = 0.99). Thus, the specific activity of a GAD protein is a function of the number of methionine residues in that protein. Immunoprecipitation of GAD protein A total of 15 yl of diluted serum (diluted 1/10 to 1/50,000) from individ- uals with stiff-man syndrome, new-onset IDDM, or from control subjects was incubated with 30 yl of metabolically-labeled GAD protein for 12 to 15 h at 4°C in a total assay volume of 60 pI (23). Based on percent incorporation rate of [3sS]methionine, 50,000 cpm of each GAD protein were used in each assay tube. All dilutions of serum and GAD protein were made with Tris-buffered saline/0.05% Tween 20 (TBST). Protein A-Sepha- rose (PAS, Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO) was washed and resus- pended in TBST to a 2% solution. Six microliters of PAS (final bed vol- ume) were added to the reaction and mixed for 1 h at 4°C with gentle rotation. Using this volume of PAS, protein A is in excess of Ig in the serum (manufacturer's specification). We confirmed experimentally that 6 yl of PAS quantitatively immunoprecipitates Igs from the serum samples. The protein A-Sepharose pellets were washed three times with 500 pl of TBST and resuspended in 100 yl of 1% SDS and counted by p scintillation counting. All samples were assayed in duplicates. The relative amount of GAD protein immunoprecipitated was expressed as a molar ratio ((cpm of GAD protein immunoprecipitated by test serum minus cpm of GAD pro- tein immunoprecipitated by normal serum)/(number of methionine residues in the GAD protein fragment)) as previously described (23). In an inter- laboratory comparison of GAD Ab assays (29), this assay was positive in 82% of sera from individuals with new-onset diabetes, 100% of bera from individuals with prediabetes, and 3% of normal individuals (1994 Work- shop results). Whether SMS or IDDM sera reacted with a particular GAD fragment was determined by assaying normal sera in parallel and from standards established during our laboratory's participation in international GAD Ab workshops (29, 30). A serum was determined to be positive for a particular GAD fragment if its reactivity exceeded the reactivity of five normal sera with that GAD fragment. Blocking of Ab binding to GAD protein Unlabeled, full length GAD 65, GAD 67, and GAD protein fragments were prepared in the reticulocyte lysate system as detailed above except that the amino acid mixture contained only unlabeled methionine. Since the me- thionine concentration in the reticulocyte lysate system is not limiting, similar amounts of cold and labeled protein were synthesized in a trans- lation reaction, diluted to the desired molar ratios, and then used for com- petition studies. For competition experiments. a dilution curve of a partic- ular serum was used to select the serum dilution in which binding to the GAD protein was easily detectable but less than maximum. Both labeled and unlabeled translation reactions were diluted five to eightfold so that the molar ratio of labeled protein to cold protein was 1 :3 to 1 :6. The molar ratio was the same for different protein fragments. For the blocking studies, unlabeled GAD protein or reticulocyte lysate without GAD protein (neg- ative control) was incubated with a dilution of the test serum for 3 h at room temperature; this mixture was then added to 50,000 cpm of ["SICAD protein and incubated for 12 to 15 h at 4°C (23). Each serum used in the blocking studies was diluted according to a previously per- formed serum dilution curve to allow for detection of Ab activity and to insure that the blocking assay was performed under the condition of Ag excess (serum dilutions ranged from 1/100 to 1/1000). PAS was added and immunoprecipitation was performed as described above. The immunopre- cipitate was analyzed by scintillation counting and expressed as the per- centage of inhibition of GAD binding following preincubation with reticu- locyte lysate without GAD protein. This approach is based on the following factors: 1) GAD 65 and GAD 67 are highly homologous in the middle and COOH-terminus of the protein; 2) GAD 65 and GAD 67 likely have similar protein conformations; 3) GAD Abs present at the highest titer in SMS and IDDM bind GAD 65 but not GAD 67. By exchanging regions of the GAD 65 and GAD 67 cDNAs, we created GAD chimeric proteins and examined the binding of GAD 65-specific Abs to a specific region of the GAD 65 protein. This approach utilized a serum dilution at which reactivity to full length GAD 65 was detectable, but reactivity to GAD 65 fragments or full length GAD 67 was not detectable. The following nomenclature for GAD chimera was utilized: GAD 65 or 67 (amino acid number of that GAD species present in the GAD chimera)/GAD 65 or 67 (amino acid number of that GAD species present in the GAD chimera). Although GAD 65 and GAD 67 differ substantially in the N- terminal third of the protein and the first eight amino acids of GAD 65 are targeted by low titer Abs in SMS sera (Fig. 2B) (19,21), the high titer, GAD 65-specific autoantibodies from SMS sera do not bind a chimeric protein consisting of the N-terminal third of GAD 65 and the C-terminal two-thirds of GAD 67 (GAD 65 (1-195)/ GAD 67 (205-594)) (Fig. 4, A and B). Both SMS and IDDM sera bind GAD 67 (1-230)/GAD 65 (221-585) (Fig. 4, A and B). An identical pattern was seen with 12 other IDDM sera (33) and is typically a larger cohort of 30 IDDM sera. The inability to detect binding to the N-terminal third of GAD 65 with IDDM sera is consistent with our prior observation that GAD 65 (188-585) is capable of blocking the binding of IDDM sera to full length GAD 65 (23). Two regions of the C-terminal two-thirds of GAD 65 are targeted by both SMS and IDDM sera. Most SMS and IDDM sera bind chimeric proteins that contain amino acids 221-442 of GAD 65 and chimeric proteins that contain amino acids 443- 585 of GAD 65 (Fig. 4, A and B and Fig. 5). One serum (SMS 4) targets GAD 65 (1-442)/GAD 67 (452-594), but not GAD 67 (1-451)/GAD 65 (443-585), which suggests the presence of only one Ab or a much higher titer of this Ab in this serum. Discussion Comparisons of the autoimmune repertoires in SMS and IDDM are important since most individuals with one disease do not de- velop the other disease despite the fact that GAD is an important autoantigen in both diseases. How an autoimmune process directed at a single protein yields two different diseases is not known. One potential explanation is that the GAD protein epitopes targeted by the immune system are different in the two diseases. The current study addresses this hypothesis by precisely characterizing and comparing epitope regions targeted by GAD autoantibodies from individuals with SMS and IDDM. Our results indicate that there are both differences and similarities in the humoral autoimmunity directed at GAD in the two diseases. Differences in the humoral response to GAD in SMS and IDDM sera Using an immunoprecipitation assay to measure anti-GAD speci- ficity, our results demonstrate that the humoral response to GAD in SMS is quite complex as reflected by the presence of several GAD autoantibody species directed at several regions of the GAD pro- tein. SMS and IDDM sera differ in titer and epitope specificity of GAD Abs All SMS sera tested contained Abs to GAD 65 and GAD 67 at titers greater than 1/1000 in distinction to IDDM sera whose GAD autoantibodies bind GAD 65 only and can rarely be detected at a greater titer than 1/100. In this fashion, individuals with SMS resemble individuals with the restricted ICA pattern (GAD-absorbable pattern) that has been associated with a low rate of progression to IDDM (23). SMS sera also have Abs that bind GAD protein fragments consisting of the amino-, middle-, and carboxyl third of the GAD 65 protein. In contrast, the autoanti- bodies in IDDM sera react predominantly with GAD 65, do not bind GAD protein fragments or with denatured GAD protein by immunoblotting, and thus target GAD protein epitopes dependent on protein conformation (10, 23). GAD Ab assay methodology Our results support previously published work on mapping of SMS epitopes, but demonstrate that prior studies have failed to discover the complexity of GAD Abs in SMS sera. The likely reason is the method of GAD Ab analysis. Our assay was optimized for mea- suring the GAD Abs found in IDDM sera and uses immunopre- cipitation of GAD protein prepared by in vitro translation. Two workshops on GAD Abs and IDDM have emphasized that immu- noprecipitation is the assay of choice in assessing IDDM-relevant GAD Abs (29, 30). Since all prior studies of SMS sera used im- munoblotting and IDDM sera do not react with GAD by immu- noblotting, no prior study can compare epitopes in these two types of sera (19, 21). Abs (monoclonal or polyclonal) often behave quite differently in immunoprecipitation and immunoblotting sys- tems and therefore our results are not directly comparable with reports using immunoblotting. However, in certain instances there is close agreement between the two assays. For example, we iden- tified an aminoterminal epitope using immunoprecipitation of a GAD fragment containing amino acids 1-195 and this is in perfect agreement with the findings by immunoblotting (19, 21). An alternative interpretation to the presence of two different types of GAD Abs (one that binds a conformation-dependent epitope and a second type that binds linear epitopes) is that there exists a single type of Ab having a different affinity for full length GAD protein and GAD protein fragments. The apparent difference in the titer of Abs dependent on protein conformation and Abs that bind denatured GAD protein may simply reflect an Ab species with a higher affinity for the full length protein than for the dena- tured protein. The current study cannot distinguish between these two interpretations since SMS sera reflect a polyclonal response to several epitope regions of the GAD protein. Further understanding of the affinity/titer of a particular Ab species requires the isolation of mAb species. The use of total human serum to map autoantibody epitopes may be problematic because: 1) the immune response to an autoantigen is likely to be polyclonal; 2) different autoantibodies may be present at different titers; 3) the reactivity of a certain autoantibody cannot be examined in isolation; and 4) the inability to discrimi- nate between a single Ab species directed at an epitope and a set of closely-related Abs targeting the same epitope. A precise quan- titation of the number of autoantibodies in a particular serum and mAb approach, all lymphocyte clones are immortalized and the contribution of a particular clone, which may be present at an extremely low frequency, to the total GAD autoantibody pool is difficult to discern. The relative frequency of a particular autoan- tibody species in relation to other autoantibody species can only be answered with total serum. In the current study, we have focused on particular GAD autoantibodies by using several GAD autoan- tigens and several dilutions of serum. The use of full length GAD protein, GAD protein fragments, and GAD peptides as targets in our assay allows one to focus on those Ab species that optimally bind that target. For example, using a 1/10,000 dilution of SMS sera, reactivity to only full length GAD protein is detected. In contrast, SMS serum at a dilution of 1/100 contains not only such full length, conformation-dependent Abs but also Abs that bind GAD protein fragments. If one uses GAD protein fragments or GAD peptides with SMS serum at a 1/100 dilution, then only Abs capable of reacting with GAD protein fragments are detected, even though this serum dilution would contain Abs that bind full length GAD protein. Imdications for oathogenesis of SMS and IDDM Differences in GAD epitope recognition in SMS and IDDM have led to speculation that differences in the autoimmune repertoire are important in determining whether diabetes or neurologic symp- toms develop ( 19, 21). Our observation that GAD Abs in both SMS and IDDM are similar does not support this hypothesis, but additional studies are required to determine if these SMS and IDDM Abs target precisely the same amino acids or the same regions of the protein. The lower titer GAD Abs in SMS that bind linear epitopes may represent immunologic spreading of an ex- tremely vigorous humoral response from conformation-dependent GAD epitopes in SMS. The failure to detect such Abs in IDDM may simply reflect the much lower titer of GAD Abs in this dis- ease. Perhaps, if a comparable level of GAD Abs from IDDM and SMS sera were examined, GAD Abs that target linear epitopes would be found in IDDM. The findings of Richter et al. support this view since they isolated mAbs capable of immunoblotting GAD from an individual with IDDM (22). The presence of the SMS-specific Abs in individuals with SMS alone and also in individuals with SMS and IDDM also suggests that the difference in GAD epitope recognition in these two disease is not the explanation for why IDDM or SMS develops. Since up to one-third of individuals with SMS also have diabetes, it is as- sumed that the pathogenesis of the diabetes in SMS is an autoim- mune process that is comparable to IDDM in the absence of SMS. If differences in the humoral response influence whether IDDM or SMS develops, then one would expect the GAD Ab profile to differ in individuals with SMS and IDDM and individuals with SMS alone. Kim et al. found that Abs directed at a linear, N-terminal epitope distinguished SMS from IDDM sera. However, both SMS patients with and without IDDM had Abs directed at this epitope and no epitope pattern distinguished individuals who also had IDDM. This conclusion is supported by our findings that the two individuals with SMS and diabetes targeted epitope regions similar to those of the two individuals with SMS alone.
