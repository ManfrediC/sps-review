Meinck (I~) Department of Clinical Neurophysiology, University of Heidelberg, Im Neuenheimer Feld 400, D-69120 Heidelberg, Germany Abstract The neurophysiological findings in eight patients with the stiff man syndrome (SMS), including four of six tested with autoantibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase, are presented. Neurophysiological findings did not make it possible to discriminate between patients with and those without autoimmunity against GABAergic neurons. Investi- gation of mono-and polysynaptic re- flexes revealed abnormal results in a variable number of SMS patients, the abnormalities largely corresponding to those seen in spastic paresis. A stereotyped motor response to elec- trical stimulation of peripheral nerves was recorded from the trunk muscles of all patients investigated. This response was termed spasmodic reflex myoclonus and consisted of a sequence of 1-3 synchronous my- oclonic bursts, 60-70 ms after me- dian nerve stimulation, followed by a tonic decrescendo activity over a number of seconds. The recruitment order of muscles along the neuraxis in spasmodic reflex myoclonus sug- gested that the latter was generated in the spinal cord and conveyed via propriospinal tracts. It is thought that spasmodic reflex myoclonus may serve not only as a diagnostic tool, but also as a key to understanding some aspects of the pathophysiology of both spasms and stiffness in SMS. It is speculated that stiffness is a fragment of spasms, both being gen- erated by common neuronal mecha- nisms tentatively ascribed to in- terneurons in the spinal grey matter. Key words Stiff man syndrome Myoclonus - Spasticity • Rigidity Introduction Stiff man syndrome (SMS) is a motor disturbance charac- terized by fluctuating muscular rigidity predominantly of the trunk muscles on which are superimposed painful spontaneous or induced spasms [23]. Rigidity corre- sponds in the electromyogram (EMG) to a tonic firing of motor units [7], and both rigidity and spasms favourably respond to medication with the benzodiazepines [11]. Un- til now, mainly single cases subjected to a variety of elec- trophysiological investigations have been reported and the pathophysiology and the locus of the disturbance has re- mained unclear. The recent discovery of autoantibodies directed against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) in over 50% of patients raised the possibility that autoimmu- nity directed against GABAergic neurons may play a pathogenetic role [25]. Here we present neurophysiologi- cal findings relevant to both diagnosis and pathophysiol- ogy of SMS, obtained from eight patients who were diag- nosed and examined according to identical clinical and methodological standards [22]. This paper extends earlier reports based on observations in one patient (case 1; [18, 19]). Some preliminary findings have been published elsewhere [16, 17]. Patients and methods We report on eight patients, five female and three male, whose clinical data and findings including results of electroencephalogra- 135 phy (EEG), electromyography (EMG), evoked potential and blink reflex testing as well as magnetic brain stimulation have been re- ported recently; patient numbers in this paper correspond to those in the previous report [22]. Neurophysiological investigations comprised EEG-EMG- polygraphy and reflex EMG, both being performed mainly with surface electrodes (5 x 5 mm) longitudinally fixed 1-2 cm apart over the muscle belly. If cross-talk from neighbouring muscles was a problem, concentric needle electrodes were placed intramus- cularly at sites not evoking pain. Back-averaging of the EEG locked to spontaneous jerks or spasms did not yield acceptable re- suits owing to prominent artefacts in the EEG. Reflex EMG in- cluded investigations of T, H, long loop, and tibialis anterior flexor reflexes, T reflexes were elicited by tapping the tendon with a hammer equipped with a microswitch. T and H reflexes were elicited in the triceps surae muscle by 1-ms square wave shocks delivered to the tibial nerve in the popliteal fossa at 10-s intervals. H reflexes were used to investigate H/M ratio, post-reflex silent period, vibration-induced reflex inhibition, and low-frequency re- flex depression. H reflex data were compared with those obtained from 15 healthy age-matched subjects. The H/M ratio was calculated by dividing the maximum H re- flex by the maximum M wave amplitudes, and ranged in the con- trol group between 0.1 and 0.7. The silent period was elicited by a shock just suprathreshold for motor fibres applied to the tibial nerve in the popliteal fossa during sustained moderate inuervation (10-20% of maximum effort) of the triceps surae muscle; it ranged between 82 and 178 ms in the normal controls. The vibratory inhi- bition of the H reflex [5] was investigated by repeatedly eliciting series of 15 H reflexes each, and applying a sustained vibration (100 Hz; 1 mm) to the Achilles tendon between the 5th and 1 lth reflexes of each series. At least two trials were performed, and the mean amplitude of the H reflexes during vibration was expressed as the percentage of the mean amplitude before vibration. In the control group, the vibratory inhibition ranged between 26% and 64%. Low-frequency depression [13] was investigated by eliciting series of 10 H reflexes, each separated by at least 40 s, at succes- sively shorter stimulus intervals (10, 2, 1, 0.2, and 0.1 s). The mean amplitude of the last three reflexes of each series was expressed as a percentage of amplitude of the first reflex. Long loop reflexes [6] were elicited in the abductor pollicis brevis muscle by repetitive (2/s) stimulation of the median and su- perfical radial nerves at the wrist during sustained moderate inner- ration. The surface EMG was full-wave rectified, and 256 reflex responses were summated. Results were compared with those ob- tained from 52 healthy controls. The flexor reflex of the resting tibialis anterior muscle [20, 21] was evoked by trains of five rec- tangular 0.1-ms pulses separated by 5 ms each, delivered to the medial plantar nerve at intervals of 10, 5, 2, 1, and 0.5 s. Eight con- secutive reflex responses were summated after full-wave rectifica- tion. Normal values were obtained from 41 healthy subjects. Three methodological problems might be mentioned: 1. Patients were seen in several institutions, and the majority of data were recorded on-line. 2. Investigations could not be performed in all patients under drug-free conditions. This problem will be discussed in some detail below. 3. Some investigations could not be performed extensively as they caused considerable discomfort to patients. EMG spasms were highly variable with respect to their localization, duration, and intensity. In two patients, jerky and violent spontaneous spasmodic attacks, clinically identical to those repeatedly seen before, oc- curred during an EMG examination. In patient 3, the EMG disclosed short bursts at irregular intervals, synchronous in the abdominal and paraspinal muscles, with a frequency gradually increasing from l-2/s to 8-10/s, and with a rapid decay after about 1 rain (Fig. 1, middle panel). In pa- tient 5, a series of 38 consecutive spasms was recorded, each initiated with 1-6 short burst discharges at intervals between 76 and 122 ms followed by a tonic decrescendo activity for up to 3 s. Bursts most often started from the right (16/38) and left (10/38) gastrocnemius muscles and successively spread into the neighbouring leg muscles on both sides, and to the shoulder muscles in 12 of 38 records, the deltoid burst being delayed to the corresponding burst in the lumbar paraspinal muscles by 19-51 ms (mean: 31.7 ms; Fig. 1, bottom panel). In all patients, acoustic and a wide variety of so- matosensory, but not visual, stimuli increased tonic mo- tor unit firing and eventually provoked spasms, often with a jerky onset. The receptive field for a given re- sponse largely depended on the stimulus strength, but principally covered the whole body. Spasms in one mus- cle were also provoked by voluntary activation in an- other one. Such "overflow" spasms (see [10]) were eas- ily precipitated by activation of neighbouring muscles, but also occurred with forcible innervation of remote muscles clinically not involved, or even with a cough. In general, induced spasmodic activity had a more jerky on- set and a shorter duration than spontaneous spasms. Moreover, there was some association between the stim- ulus strength and the characteristics of the responses. Discussion Our investigation of eight patients with SMS revealed ab- normalities of segmental and suprasegmental reflex trans- mission seemingly related to the pathophysiology of spas- tic motor disturbances. Although clinical or electrophysi- ological signs normally associated with spasticity were absent in our patients, except for hyper-reflexia of the legs, some pathophysiological relationship might be de- rived from the therapeutic efficacy of antispastic drugs such as baclofen, tizanidine, or the benzodiazepines [7, 11, 18, 22]. High H/M ratios in the triceps surae muscle associated with abnormally poor development of both low-frequency depression and vibration-induced inhibi- tion of the corresponding H reflex were seen in two of five patients (Fig. 6). Poor low-frequency depression as well as reduced vibratory inhibition have been described previously in spastic limbs [5, 13]. Among our patients, abnormal monosynaptic reflex mech- anisms occurred irrespective of autoimmunity against GABAergic neurons. However, as vibratory inhibition was reported to be abnormal in two other patients [12, 15], this test might provide important clues in future neurophys- iological analysis of SMS if applied more systematically. Long loop reflexes to the hand muscles in patients 1, 3, and 4 provided evidence not only of exaggerated seg- mental reflex mechanisms (corresponding to clinical symptoms in the arms of patients 1 and 4), but also of dis- turbed transcortical reflex mediation [6]. As median nerve evoked cortical potentials proved normal, this might be ascribed to the central relays of the reflex loop or to the corticospinal tract, which contrasts a lack of pyramidal symptoms and normal corticomuscular latencies [22]. Flexor reflexes in the tibialis anterior muscle were dis- tinctly abnormal in a high proportion of patients (Fig. 7), irrespective of their immunological status or drug treat- ment. Both the alteration of the reflex pattern as well as its abnormal temporal facilitation closely correspond to ab- normalities seen in spastic legs. This finding presumably in both spasticity and SMS is due to impaired control of segmental mechanisms [20]. It is noteworthy that the ab- normal temporal facilitation of the flexor reflex contrasts with the profound habituation of spasmodic reflex my- oclonus (Fig. 5 A). This suggests that these two types of cutaneomuscular reflexes are generated in different neu- ronal networks. Our polymyographic analysis was based on the obser- vation that tonic firing of motor units in SMS is distinctly susceptible to external influences which, if sufficiently brisk or intense, cause generalized tonic or jerky spasms [7]. The latter are termed here spasmodic reflex myo- clonus as: 1. Their onset is jerky and violent, i.e. myoclonic, con- sisting of a series of 1-3 synchronized, large, and rapid EMG bursts. 2. Their end is gradually waning, i.e. spasmodic, with a tonic decrescendo EMG activity. 3. In a given patient, their EMG pattern is stereotypically evoked with onset latencies around 60-80 ms, i.e. it is a reflex (Fig. 2). The EMG pattern of spasmodic reflex myoclonus corre- sponds to the one recorded from the leg muscles in patient 5 during a series of violent spontaneous jerky spasms (Fig. 1, bottom recording), which suggests that both spontaneous spasms and spasmodic reflex myoclonus are of a similar nature, at least in this case. Both cortical and reticular reflex myoclonus as well as some of the hyperekplexias are associated with EEG and evoked potential abnormalities, suggesting enhanced cortical excitability [2, 8, 9, 14, 24, 27]. Some clinical re- semblance with exaggerated startle responses (e.g. in fa- milial hyperekplexia) does indeed exist. However, muscle recruitment in both physiological startle and hyperek- plexia is down from the fifth and seventh cranial motor nuclei to the spinal cord [2]. In spasmodic reflex my- oclonus, the polymyographic recordings (Figs. 3-5) sug- gest a slow spread of the EMG responses from muscles of spinal cord segments C6/7 concomitantly up to the brain stem and down to the lumbosacral motor nuclei. A simple explanation for this concomitant spread in both directions, and on both sides, along the neuraxis is that the excitatory volley is conveyed within a bilateral longitudinal spinal cord system. A particular form of myoclonic jerking pre- dominantly of the axial muscles was recently suggested to arise in propriospinal systems [3, 4]. Indeed, proprio- spinal myoclonus in spinal cord diseases and spasmodic reflex myoclonus in SMS share several features, such as long duration, jerky components, and slow bi-directional spread on both sides. Propriospinal myoclonus, however, seems to have a strong flexor dominance and a poor stim- ulus sensitivity, both features contrasting with the spas- modic reflex myoclonus seen in SMS. Median nerve stimulation in two cases led to responses with the shortest latencies not in the cervical but in the mid-thoracic paraspinal muscles (Figs. 3 B, 4 A). This may indicate that in these patients the brunt of abnormal ex- citability is located in the latter segments of the spinal cord, and one might speculate as to whether the median nerve volley at this site is relayed to the ascending and de- scending fibre tracts. However, it more likely shows that the onset latency of spasmodic reflex myoclonus strongly depends on the gain of synaptic transmission, which is presumably highest in the spinal cord segments most se- verely afflicted, but may considerably differ at various levels of the neuraxis. Such a differential gain in the reflex transmission to different spinal cord or brain stem motor nuclei would inevitably invalidate any calculation of prop- agation velocities of the excitatory volley in the spinal cord. Nevertheless, this does not principally invalidate this technique [9], as the results are fairly consistent be- tween individual patients (Fig.4A) and are, moreover, compatible with a second type of testing which compares the overall conduction times with median and tibial nerve stimulation to a gi~ien motor nucleus (Figs. 3 C, D, 4B). An important feature of spasmodic reflex myoclonus is its regular appearance in the lower trunk muscles of all patients tested, and its absence in other diseases with mus- cular hypertonia or myoclonic jerks, or both, as well as in normal subjects. As we are not aware of any observation of this particular type of reflex myoclonus in other motor disorders, it may be considered characteristic of SMS and thus serve as an electrophysiological marker, allowing one, for example, to differentiate spasmodic reflex myoclonus in SMS from psychogenic jerks [26]. Obviously, this marker is independent of the presence or absence of GAD auto- antibodies [25], but its electrophyisological appearance may be influence by drug treatment. With regard to the variety of mechanisms triggering spasms and spasmodic reflex myoclonus from a wide re- ceptive field, one might speculate as to whether stiffness, spasms and spasmodic reflex myoclonus are generated by 142 common neural mechanisms, the differential manifesta- tions of motor excitability being patterned by the shape, intensity and duration of the afferent signals. Stiffness might in fact be considered a fragment of spasmodic re- flex myoclonus elicited by asynchronous afferent im- pulses from, for example, skin or muscle receptors. Some general characteristics of such common neuronal mecha- nisms are that they are subject to a rapid and mutual ha- bituation, and that they apparently receive convergence form a wide variety of supraspinal and spinal sources, sug- gesting important integrative functions. Among the for- mer, low-threshold muscle afferents, which are strongly controlled by the GABAergic presynaptic inhibition (see [5]) were not as particularly effective in evoking motor hyperactivity (Fig. 5 B) as might be expected in the pres- ence of autoimmunity against GABAergic neurons [25]. In fact, electrical stimulation of the median or tibial nerves often evoked H reflexes in the corresponding muscles without concomitant spasms or myoclonic jerks; the latter were, however, evoked with increasing stimuli to the same nerves or to their cutaneous branches. According to clinical and microscopical evidence previously discussed [22], the spinal grey matter and the adjacent propriospinal tracts may be regarded as the prime candidates for the lo- cus of the disturbance, the neurons in the spinal grey mat- ter having indeed prominent integrative functions [1].
