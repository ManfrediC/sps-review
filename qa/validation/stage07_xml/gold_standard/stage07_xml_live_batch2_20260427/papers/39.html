Stiff-man syndrome is a rare neurological disorder characterized by skeletal muscle rigidity and spasms in which detection of circulating anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies has suggested an autoimmune pathogenesis. TO further define the role of autoimmunity in the pathogenesis, we studied anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies, as well as organ- and non-organ-specific autoantibodies in 13 patients with stiff-man syndrome and 127 patients with other neurological disorders. Thyrogastric antibodies were more frequent in patients with stiff-man syndrome (46%) than in those with other neurological disorders (12%) (p < 0.05). Non-organ-specific antibodies were found at a similar frequency in the patients with stiff-man syndrome (61%) and those with other neurological disorders (65%). Islet-cell autoantibodies and anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies were more common in stiff-man syndrome patients (38% and 31%) compared to the patients with other neurological disorders (6% and 396, respectively; fi < 0.001). With the exception of 1 patient in the other neurological disorders group, anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies were always associated with islet-cell autoantibodies. Four patients with stiff-man syndrome had an associ- ated solid tumor: 3 of them had antibodies recognizing a 125/130-kd protein and not glutamic acid decarboxylase. Our study indicates that with immunological markers, patients with stiff-man syndrome can be subdivided into three groups: (1) patients with circulating islet-cell autoantibodies, anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase as well as other organ-specific autoantibodies, and the frequent association with an autoimmune disease (autoimmune variant); (2) patients with associated neoplasms and circulating non-organ-specific autoantibodies but neither islet-cell nor anti- glutamic acid decarboxylase autoantibodies (paraneoplastic variant); and (3) patients with no evidence of any known autoantibody or association with other clinically evident diseases (idiopathic variant). We conclude that the presence of anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies is restricted to a subgroup of stiff-man syndrome patients with associated autoimmune diseases. The absence of these antibodies does not exclude a diagnosis of stiff-man syndrome, which is still based on clinical and neurophysiological evidence. Ann Neurol 1993;34:57-64 Stiff-man syndrome (SMS), a rare neurological disor- der first described by Moersch and Woltman in 1956 111, is characterized by painful persistent axial and proximal limb muscle rigidity, which is aggravated by sensory stimuli and abolished by sleep and anesthesia El]. Electromyography of affected muscles at rest shows spontaneous constant firing of normal motor units in a full recruitment pattern, reinforced by pas- sive and voluntary movements {2]. The muscle con- traction tends to become chronic and leads the patient to a state of constant rigidity that is only relieved by systemic administration of benzodiazepines or other y-aminobutyric acid (GABA) ag-onists 12-41, The etiopathogenesis of SMS is still obscure. Recent studies [S, 61 demonstrated circulating autoantibodies directed against the GABAergic nerve terminals in up to 60% of SMS patients, and suggested an autoim- mune-mediated impairment of GABAergic neuro- transmission due to autoreactivity to the GABA- converting enzyme glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD). Moreover, the existence of a group of anti-GAD antibody-negative, otherwise typical SMS patients r9- 1 11 further questions the pathogenetic relevance of these antibodies in SMS. We previously reported the presence of anti-GAD, organ-, and non-organ-specific antibodies in IDDM patients, blood donors, and patients with nonneurolog- ical autoimmune disorders 17). Discussion The finding of anti-GAD antibodies in a proportion of SMS patients has suggested an autoimmune-mediated pathogenesis of this rare disorder {5, 6). Accordingly, an autoantibody-mediated attack against GABAergic transmission may impair the cerebellar control of mus- cle tone. However, the presence of circulating anti- GAD antibodies in a large proportion of IDDM pa- tients without clinical or electrophysiological signs of motor-unit hyperactivity [7) questions the relevance of anti-GAD antibodies in the pathogenesis of SMS. GAD is the key enzyme in the synthesis of GABA, the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain. GAD has also been found in pancreatic p-cells and at lower levels, in the ludneys, liver, adrenal glands, ovary, and testes [lS). Two closely related GAD iso- forms (with molecular masses of 59 and 67 kd) have been identified in the brain and in the pancreas. The two isoforms are encoded by two different genes, have different intracellular distributions, and show different functional properties rib- 191. While the role of GABA in the brain has been established, little is known about the role of GAD and GABA in other tissues. GAD was recently identified as the 64-kd autoanti- gen recognized by sera from IDDM patients [20, 21) and is also a major target of islet-cell autoantibodies 171, a highly predictive marker of IDDM 122). Sera positive for islet-cell autoantibodies are classified ac- cording to the selectivity of their islet-cell staining [23]: A first subgroup carries GAD-specific, p-cell- restricted islet-cell autoantibodies, while a second sub- group shows reactivity against antigens, other than GAD, present in all endocrine cells within pancreatic islets. It is unknown whether an immune-mediated attack against a brain GAD could account for the occurrence of SMS, since it is not clear whether GAD can also undergo posttranslational mod- hcation and become membrane-bound in the brain Ela. Thus, SMS and IDDM share GAD as a major au- toantigen. However, it is unknown what causes the appearance of one disease over the other, which are the cofactor(s) involved in tissue specificity of these autoantibodies, and why SMS is so much rarer than IDDM. In an attempt to find immunological features differ- entiating autoantibodies seen in IDDM and SMS pa- tients, we evaluated the autoantibody pattern of 13 SMS patients with particular emphasis on anti-GAD antibodies. As others have reported L5, 61, we found anti-GAD antibodies only in a subset of patients with clinically diagnosed SMS. In our series, anti-GAD anti- bodies were found in 4 (3 1 %) of 13 patients with SMS. Whether the similarity in anti-GAD antibody lev- els, between the two subgroups of SMS and IDDM patients, reflects a common pathogenetically meaning- ful event is presently unknown. We found evidence of autoimmune activation in two-thirds of patients with SMS. Support to a specific GAD autoantibody-mediated pathogenesis was ob- tained only in half of these patients (usually female patients with a diffuse polyendocrine autoreactivity). In the remaining half, the latter mechanism could not be advocated: Serum samples from the 4 SMS patients with an associated neoplasm, for example, did not show anti-GAD antibodies. Instead, they strongly recog- nized a brain protein of 1251130 kd. It is tempting to speculate that these patients represent a paraneoplastic subgroup. This 1251130-kd protein, as seen in other paraneoplastic autoimmune syndromes [26f, could be a constitutive component of neuronal cells able to trig- ger a strong autoimmune reaction. Characterization of this protein might lead to a better understanding of the pathogenesis underlying some cases of SMS. We also identified another subgroup of SMS patients (mainly male) without polyendocrine autoreactivity or evidence of anti-GAD antibodies. In the absence of any other clue on the nature of these patients’ illness, we presently consider them as affected by idiopathic SMS. If anti-GAD autoantibodies are not necessarily asso- ciated with SMS, how do we then explain their appear- ance in some but not all SMS patients? These auto- antibodies may represent a mere epiphenomenon unrelated, at least in the majority of SMS patients, to the pathogenesis of the disease. A pathological process against central nervous system structures rich in GAD might release normally sequestered molecules, which might become targets for an autoimmune response. Alternatively, autosensitization against GAD may actu- ally arise at the level of pancreatic @-cells and cross- react with central nervous system cells rich in GAD. This mechanism, although potentially able to cause pathological changes in all exposed individuals, does so in only a subgroup of SMS patients with other predis- posing factor(s); in fact, sporadic juvenile [27) cases of SMS or with autosomal dominant genetic [28], infec- tious (291, or paraneoplastic {7-11, 303 origin have been described. In conclusion, our study indicates that SMS is a het- erogeneous disorder and can be subdivided into three main subgroups: (1) SMS patients (mostly female) who have associated autoimmune diseases (mainly IDDM) and who test positive for islet-cell autoantibodies, anti- GAD antibodies, and organ-specific autoantibodies, and negative for non-organ-specific autoantibodies (autoimmune SMS); (2) SMS patients (mostly male) who have associated neoplasms and frequently have non-organ-specific autoantibodies, but who test nega- tive for islet-cell autoantibodies, anti-GAD antibodies, or organ-specific autoantibodies (paraneoplastic SMS); and (3) SMS patients who have no other associated clinical conditions but who show a high prevalence of non-organ-specific autoantibodies, but no islet-cell au- toantibodies, anti-GAD antibodies, or organ-specific autoantibodies (idiopathic SMS). In the paraneoplastic subgroup of SMS patients, we frequently detected an antibody directed against an unknown 125/ 130-kd protein, suggesting that central nervous system anti- gens other than GAD may be the target of autoimmu- nity in SMS patients. Our findings indicate that the diagnosis of SMS should remain clinical and that the absence of circulat- ing anti-GAD antibodies does not exclude a diagnosis of SMS. Other central nervous system molecules can be the target of an autoimmune attack in some patients with SMS. The pathogenetic relevance of these autoan- tibodies, as well as that of anti-GAD antibodies, de- serves further scrutiny. Moreover, clinical evidence of continuous motor-unit hyperactivity should alert the clinician to the possible existence of an associated neo- plasm.
