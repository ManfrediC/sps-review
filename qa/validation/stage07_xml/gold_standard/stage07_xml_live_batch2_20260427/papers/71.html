In.trathecal baclofen therapy in stiff-man syndrome: A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial P.L. Silbert, MBBS, FRACP; J.Y. Matsumoto, MD; P.G. McManis, MBBS, FRACP; K.A. Stolp-Smith, MD; B.A. Elliott, MD; and K.M. McEvoy, MD, PhD Article abstract-We performed a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of intrathecal baclofen (ITB) in stiff-man syndrome. Three patients, unresponsive to current therapy, received 50 pg of ITB or placebo on sequential days. Following ITB, all patients demonstrated improvement in reflex EMG activity. The mean reduction in total EMG activity (from all muscles) following stimulation of the medial plantar nerve (cutaneous flexor reflex) was 72% following 50 pg of ITB compared with 18% following placebo (ANOVA: significance of F, p < 0.0001). The mean latency to onset of the response was also significantly prolonged for all muscles following ITB (ANOVA: significance of F, p < 0.05). Although reflex EMG activity was reduced in all patients, clinical improvement was evident in only one patient, who differed from the others studied by a longer duration of disease, greater severity of stiffness, less fear of falling, and greater electrophysiologic improvement. NEUROLOGY 1995;45:1893-1897 Stiff-man syndrome (SMS) is a r a r e disorder characterized by involuntary stiffness of axial and lower-extremity muscles with superimposed painful muscle spasms.' The etiology is unknown, but a n immune-mediated mechanism is suggested by the presence of antibodies t o glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) and the strong association with other autoimmune diseases.2 GAD catalyzes the conversion of glutamate t o GABA, and there are reduced CSF GABA levels in some patients with SMS,3 making baclofen (which acts on GABAB receptors) a logical therapy. CSF penetration of baclofen is poor following oral administration4 and, although effective in some case^,^,^ the response in SMS is often variable and inadequate.7 Intrathecal administration of baclofen avoids the high systemic levels required with oral administration because of limited CSF p e n e t r a t i ~ n CSF . ~ levels up to 50 times higher may be achieved with ?booth of the oral dose.* There are reports of intrathecal baclofen (ITB) in five cases of SMSg-llbut most trials were open, and there was no electrophysiologic monitoring of the effects of ITB. In the only reported patient with a blinded evaluation, no improvement could be documented.ll We report a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of ITB therapy in three pa- tients with SMS, with intensive clinical and electrophysiologic monitoring. Methods. Patient selection. Three patients with SMS were recruited for a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of ITB therapy. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. Patients were selected because of inadequate control on their current therapy due to lack of efficacy or side effects. Symptoms of SMS had been present for 6 years in patient 1, 4 years in patient 2, and 10 years in patient 3, and these patients were currently on combinations of diazepam, clonazepam, baclofen, valproate, and prednisone. All patients fulfilled the clinical and electrophysiologic diagnostic criteria of Lorish et a17 and had positive anti-GAD antibodies. All patients had a n increased lumbar lordosis, reduced range of lumbar movement, paraspinal muscle hypertrophy, rigid abdominal muscles, spontaneous and startle-induced spasms, and at least a partial response to benzodiazepine therapy. Lower-extremity reflexes were brisk in all patients, with asymmetry of the stiffness and reflexes in patients 2 and 3. Informed consent was obtained and current medications were continued. The protocol allowed for patients to receive blinded intrathecal administration of either 50 pg of baclofen or placebo on day 1, with the other medication given on day 2. Following this, patients were eligible to receive a further trial of either 75 or 100 pg of ITB with placebo control, as determined by the patient's subjective response and the observed clinical response to the lower dose. These changes were not statistically significant. Discussion. Despite our small sample size, we were able t o detect a significant electrophysiologic response in SMS following ITB as evidenced by the reduction in total reflex EMG activity and prolongation of the onset latency of the cutaneous flexor reflex. The cutaneous flexor reflex is one of several exteroceptive reflexes consistently exaggerated in SMS.12J3J5Presumably, these reflex abnormalities reflect hyperexcitability along polysynaptic reflex arcs, most prominently at the spinal level. We observed maximal reflex EMG activity following medial plantar nerve stimulation, and found that this reflex could be reliably measured without habituation during the testing protocol, making it an excellent physiologic measure of SMS. Other electrophysiologic techniques are of limited value in evaluating the effects of treatments in SMS. Monosynaptic reflex arcs are usually normal,12 and although documentation of continuous muscle activOctober 1995 NEUROLOGY 45 1895 ity is useful in the initial evaluation of SMS, it may be absent in rested patients on medical therapy. This was apparent to us during the pretrial evaluation of our patients, and for this reason we did not employ this technique during the trial. Two aspects of the electrophysiologic response in our patients bear further comment. The reflex EMG activity was most markedly suppressed in the legs and paraspinal muscles, consistent with the known gradient of distribution of ITB following lumbar intrathecal administration.EThis pattern of response may also, i n part, reflect the known greater hyperexcitability of t r u n k a n d lowerextremity muscles in SMS.15 The prolongation of t h e onset latency i n t h e proximal leg a n d paraspinal muscles cannot be explained with certainty, but most likely reflects reduced temporal summation along the flexor reflex pathways due to baclofen’s effects on presynaptic inhibition and postsynaptic neuronal excitability.16J7 Together, these findings suggest that ITB was acting in a local and inhibitory manner at the spinal level. Although muscle stiffness was reduced in all patients, we were unable to document a statistically significant change. This may be due to a combination of the small sample size and lack of sensitivity of the Ashworth scale. Although we observed an electrophysiologic response to ITB in all patients, only one responded with clear clinical improvement. This patient was more severely affected, had asymmetric involvement of the lower extremities, had a longer duration of disease, and also showed greater electrophysiologic improvement following ITB. An insufficient dose, or a n early peak CSF level of ITB, may account for the lesser electrophysiologic improvement in the two patients who did not respond, but both patients reported increased unsteadiness of their legs and more difficulty walking following ITB, despite a reduction in muscle stiffness. We interpret these changes as treatment effects, and, together with the patients’ “fear of falling,” we felt that, following unblinding of the first stage of the trial, a higher dose of ITB would not benefit these patients. In these patients (and in future trials), further evaluation of the effects of ITB would best be performed using continuous intrathecal infusion through an intraspinal catheter to allow time for adaptation and to adjust the dose if necessary. Brashear and Phillips13 previously noted the discrepancy between the electrophysiologic and clinical responses. Their patient’s subjective clinical response was significantly delayed in comparison with the observed changes in reflex muscle activity and muscle stiffness. This further supports our suggestion that a period of time may be needed in these patients to allow for adaptation to changes in their muscle tone, and that the exteroceptive reflexes are a sensitive indicator of changes in reflex pathways important in the pathophysiology of SMS. In previously reported patients receiving ITB for 1896 NEUROLOGY 45 October 1995 SMS, three showed an impressive response to longterm ITB.gJOPenn and Mangierig documented a response to an open test dose of 75 pg, although the patient with advanced SMS, treated with a relatively limited dose of ITB (120 pg/d), showed longterm improvement in spasms and pain, but no functional improvement because of lower-extremity ankylosis. Ford and Fahnll reported a similar patient with advanced SMS, a relatively fixed increased lumbar lordosis, and ankylosis of the hips, who showed no improvement following doubleblind administration of bolus test doses up to 100 bg. The pattern of response in these patients, together with t h e SMS patients reported in our study, suggests that those most likely to benefit from ITB are those with moderately severe disease, but without a fixed deformity or ankylosis. Of the three patients reported in our trial, patient 3 has recently commenced continuous long-term ITB infusion and is responding well to his current dose of 730 pg/day. The other two patients have currently elected to continue their prestudy drug therapy, but may undergo further evaluation in the future using continuous infusion of ITB through an intraspinal catheter. In summary, our trial has demonstrated a significant electrophysiologic improvement in SMS following ITB. Although this supports the suggested efficacy of ITB in SMS,gJothe overall response in our patients did not always parallel the electrophysiologic improvement. Long-term clinical efficacy remains to be proven, but we believe that ITB may be efficacious in reducing the stiffness and spasms in some patients with SMS. Continuous infusion may be preferable to bolus administration for assessing the response to ITB in selected patients. Acknowledgments Bruce Aldrich provided technical assistance. Medtronic Inc. Fifteen of 17 patients (88%)had neurologic signs and symptoms referable to brainstem dysfunction, including dysarthria, cranial neuropathies, and gross motor incoordination. Tumors were located primarily in the medulla in 14 of 17 NF1 patients (82%), in contrast to the pontine tumor location in the non-NF1 population.
