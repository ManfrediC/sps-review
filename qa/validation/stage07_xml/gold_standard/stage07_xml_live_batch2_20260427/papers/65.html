Solimena Department of Cell Biology, Yale University, New Haven, Coimecticut, USA Abstract The clinical, biochemical, neuroimaging and neurophysiologi- cal findings of eight patients with stiff man syndroms (SMS) [four of six being tested with autoantibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD)] are presented. In two pa- tients (one GAD-positive, one GAD- negative), transient oculomotor dis- turbances suggested progressive en- cephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus (PERM) as differential diagnosis. The catalogue of charac- teristic clinical symptoms of SMS is extended by three new symptoms: (1) an aura-like feeling reported by five patients to precede spontaneous spasmodic attacks; (2) a stereotyped motor pattern seen in seven patients during spasmodic jerks, consisting of brief opisthotonos, stiffening of the slightly abducted legs and inversion of the plantar-flexed feet; (3) a paroxysmal fear when crossing a free space unaided, or even thinking of it. Clinical findings did not enable us to discriminate between patients tested GAD-positive or GAD-negative. Cerebrospinal fluid contained ele- vated immunoglobulin levels or cell counts, or both, in the majority of patients. Autopsy of one patient re- vealed scattered lymphocyte cuffs around leptomeningeal, intracerebral and particularly intraspinal vessels, suggesting a mild inflammatory process. Whether SMS and PERM are closely related is discussed; they are possibly both manifestations of a spectrum of encephalomyelopathies having autoimmunity against GABAergic neurons in common. Key words Stiff man syndrome Progressive encephalomyelitis Spasms • Muscle tone • Glutamic acid decarboxylase Introduction Stiff man syndrome (SMS) is a motor disturbance charac- terized by muscular rigidity and superimposed spasms [33]. Rigidity fluctuates between being almost absent and extreme and predominantly affects the trunk and proxi- mum limb muscles, but it may occasionally extend to the face, hands and feet [13]. Spasms occur spontaneously, but are elicited by a variety of internal or external events such as touch, noise and fright, and may be violent enough to fracture bones and to bend inserted pins [6, 13]. The dramatic bizarrerie of these main symptoms contrasts with the lack of neurological signs: central or peripheral weakness, pyramidal tract signs, ataxia, oculomotor dis- turbances, sensory impariment, or neuropsychological deficits are, in fact, considered suggestive of other diag- noses [13, 21]. 158 From its first description [33], SMS has been a curios- ity in neurology, both its locus and aetiology remaining enigmatic. A major problem is that most studies reported on single cases, many of them with "plus" symptoms, with a wide variety of paraclinical investigations. Here we present the clinical, biochemical, microscopical and neu- rophysiological findings from eight cases followed be- tween 4 and 28 years. Some preliminary findings have been published previously elsewhere [26-30]. Patients and methods We report on eight patients, five female and three male, whose per- sonal and clinical data are summarized in Table 1. Patients 1 and 3 have been reported previously [28-31]. Routine neurophysiological investigations included electroen- cephalography (EEG), electromyo-neurography (EMG, ENG), evoked potential testing (EP), and magnetic brain stimulation. The results of polymyographic and reflex-EMG investigations will be reported in detail in a subsequent paper. Serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were tested at 1 : 20 and 1 : 2 dilutions, respectively, for the presence of autoanti- bodies directed against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) and GABAergic terminals using an immunoperoxidase staining proce- dure described previously [44]. Western blots of total homogenate proteins of rat brain (10 pg) were incubated with serum or CSF samples at 1:250 and 1:50 dilution, respectively. Bound human immunoglobulins were detected using rabbit anti-human IgG (Organon) followed by 125I-protein A (Amersham) and autoradiog- raphy. Results The clinical picture In all patients, severe muscular stiffness and spontaneous as well as reflex-like induced violent spasms were the prominent features. Stiffness was the initial symptom of the disease in six patients, and was experienced by them (and often misdiagnosed by their physicians) as recurrent cramping, lumbago, or rheumatic aching. It was confined to the lower trunk and leg muscles in four patients and ex- tended to the neck and shoulder muscles in the others. Stiffness was superimposed in all patients by both spontaneous and induced spasms and jerks of variable in- tensity, duration and character, often experienced as throes, as a violent pull, or as a heavy blunt punch. Spasms and jerks had a tendency to recur spontaneous, occasionally for hours, at variable intervals. Spontaneous- ly spasms ranged from slow contractions of one muscle to violent whole-body jerks with a brisk onset and a succes- sive lessening over a period of seconds. In all but patient 3, generalized spasms showed a fairly stereotyped motor pattern with opisthotonos, stiffening of the slightly ab- ducted legs, and inversion of the extended feet, most often with only minor joint excursions probably due to co-con- traction of antagonists. The arms, if involved, were ad- ducted, stiffened and pronated. In patient 3, the same mo- tor pattern evolved out of a series of brief and synchro- nous jerks of the dorsal and ventral lower trunk muscles, gradually increasing in frequency until a tonic attitude was reached, which abruptly decayed. Similar jerks and spasms were regularly evoked by acoustic and various somatosensory stimuli to almost all parts of the body including the face, i.e. regardless of whether or not the body parts were afflicted by the dis- ease. In general, there was a good stimulus-response rela- tionship: the more abrupt and intense the stimulus, the more consistent, jerky and long-lasting was the response. Spasms and jerks were also evoked by negative (but not positive) emotions such as anger or fright as well as by brisk self-paced movements of the arms and hands, or even a cough - most specifically, however, by attempting to sit up or to bend forward. However, after both induced or spontaneous spasms and jerks there was a state of unre- sponsiveness to any kind of provocation lasting some 30 s. Spontaneous spasms in five patients were precipitated by a particular feeling of the imminent attack for seconds or even hours (patient 3), associated in patients 3, 4 and 6 with increasing fear and restlesness. Severe spontaneous spasms often occurred accompanied by intense fear and vegetatitve crises. Profuse sweating, tachycardia, arterial hypertension, widening of the pupils, or tachypnoea, or a combination, were seen in seven patients. The summary of clinical findings (Table 1) shows that axial stiffness and spasms were associated almost regu- larly with exaggerated deep tendon reflexes, gait distur- bance and a history of unexplained falls without loss of consciousness, leading in six patients to severe bruises, dislocations, or even fractures. All patients were tem- porarily misdiagnosed as hysterical, apparently because of three factors: (1) lessening of stiffness and spasms with distraction; (2) discrepancy between dramatic symptoms and lack of neurological signs; (3) paroxysmal fear (six patients) typically associated with crossing a square or a corridor unaided. Even considering this particular situa- tion was enough to evoke intense fear and consecutive spasms and falls. This was occasionally misunderstood as agoraphobic anxiety neurosis. However, fear was dimin- ished instantaneously when the patient used a crutch or had the support of an assistant. Patients (5 and 6) did not meet rigid diagnostic criteria for SMS ([13[; Table l). In both, the disease began acutely 159 with nystagmus or oculomotor disturbances and vertgio. After 3 weeks or 4 years, respectively, spasms or jerks fol- lowed and preceded the development of intense rigidity. Both patients had transient paraesthesia and both endured the most severe stiffness and the longest-lasting spas- modic attacks leading to inward dislocation of their ankle joints. The condition of patient 5 stabilized after 6 months of progression. Patient 6 had three subactue bouts, each with dramatic intensification of both stiffness and spasms, and with slow recovery. However, her additional symp- toms remained stable, and no new symptoms were noted on follow-up. Both patients may be considered as having progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and my- oclonus (PERM) because of their accessory symptoms, although the latter were mainly transitory. Symptoms developed insidiously in six cases, with slow progression over months (two cases) or even years (four cases) followed by up to 23 years (patient 1) with virtually no progression (six cases). Patients 2 and 6 had superimposed phases of deterioration, each resulting in some permanent worsening. Three patients died, two of them from SMS. At age 48, patient 2 suffered respiratory arrest during a spasmodic attack, and died 5 days later presumably from septic shock. Patient 4 died aged 32 years, a few days after acute cardiac failure during benzo- diazepine withdrawal. Patient 5 died aged 60 years from lung metastases of mesopharyngeal cancer diagnosed 1.5 years after the onset of SMS. Neuroimaging Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain disclosed multiple small signal enhancements in the subcortical white matter of the cerebral hemispheres in patient 1, and thick caps on the frontal horns in patients 3 [29], neither enhancing with paramagnetic constrast media and stable during a 6-year follow-up. MRI showed moderate atrophy of the cerebellar and frontal cerebral cortex in patient 5, but was normal in patients 4 and 6-8. In patient 2 neither MRI nor cranial computed tomography were available; however, full-length myelography was normal. MRI of the spinal cord was normal in patients 1 and 3-8, although cervical stenosis was seen in patients 1 and 7, associated in the latter with congenital block vertebrae C3-C5 and enlarged cauda equina. Anatomical investigations Muscle (patients 1 and 4) and rectum biopsies (patient 4) showed normal tissues. Post-mortem examination of pa- tient 4, who died with signs of dissociated brain death, re- vealed recent coagulation necrosis of the liver and kid- neys, superficial erosions within the gastrointestinal tract, dilatation of the heart and alveolar oedema of the lung. The brain and spinal cord showed macroscopical signs of intravital brain death. Slices (5 ~tm) fiom paraffin-embed- ded blocks of the frontal, parietal, occipital and temporal cortex, as well as hippocampus, basal ganglia, pons, medulla oblongata and seven levels of the spinal cord, were stained by haematoxylin- and eosin as well as with van Gieson and K1/Jver-Barrera stains. All grey matter re- gions showed severe ischaemic lesions combined with ex- cessive oedematous changes in the subcortical white mat- ter and the inner layer of the cortex, accentuated in the hippocampal area and in the thalamus. In the latter, oedema was accompanied by recent erythrodiapedesis. In the substantia nigra zona compacta, melanin granules in glia cells were increased, but not associated with a defi- nite loss of nerve cells. Scattered lymphocyte cuffs around leptomeningeal and intracerebral vessels, accentuated in the spinal cord, suggested a moderate inflammatory process (Fig. 1) which appeared older than the recent is- chaemic lesions. In the spinal cord, the anterior horn cells showed shrunkened perikarya and hyperchromatic nuclei, or the pattern of ischaemic nerve cell lesions with eosinophilic cytoplasm. The latter pattern was dominant in Clarke's columns. Within the anterior and lateral horn columns the number of nerve cells appeared moderately reduced. Mildly increased density of astrocytic nuclei suggested an old glia reaction without recent proliferative cell forms. The myelinated tracts and the cerebral white matter were free from demyelination. A sole fresh oede- matous sponginess was observed in the posterior subpial parts of the spinal cord. Al- though fasting blood sugar was normal in all patients, oral glucose tolerance had been abnormal in patients and 1 and 160 3 since 1979 and 1980, respectively, and was borderline in patients 6 and 7. Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus be- came manifest in patient 3 in 1988. Serological investigations for viral or bacterial agents (including tropical infections in patient 4) were repeatedly negative. The tetanus antitoxin level in patient 4 was in the protective range. A wide variety of serological markers for autoimmune diseases were negative in all cases except for patient 5, whose Westergren sedimentation rates were be- tween 73 and 97 mm in 1 h, and C-reactive protein was positive. Indirect immunofluorescence of human Ig (G) binding to rat spinal cord slices with both plasma and CSF of patient 5 yielded merely diffuse staining. By immuno- cytochemistry on rat brain frozen sections autoantibodies directed against GABAergic neurons were detected in both serum and CSF samples from patients 3 and 6-8. By immunoblot on total homogenates of rat brain both serum and CSF samples of patients 3 and 6-8 recognized a band with the same electrophoretic mobility of GAD. This band was neither recognized by, nor were autoantibodies against GABAergic neurons detected in, either the serum or the CSF samples from patients 1 and 5 (Table 1). Therapy A wide variety of drugs was tried for symptomatic treat- ment, among which the benzodiazepines proved most ef- fective. Table 3 shows, however, that the functional capa- bilities were good or satisfactory in only five patients, but were poor in three patients. Patient 2 was the only one who, at the summit of her disease, did not sufficiently re- spond even to 200 mg/day of oral diazepam. Moreover, therapy with benzodiazepines was accompanied by seri- ous side-effects. After 2 years of clonazepam medication, patient 4 was considered clonazepam-addicted; he as well as patient 1 suffered grand mal seizures, and patient 3 delirium, after reduction of the benzodiazepine dosage. In patients 5, 6 and 7, the beneficial effects of the benzodi- azepines on stiffness and spasms were limited because of sedation, double vision, dysarthria, or ataxia. The latter sup- pressed stiffness, spasms and jerks in both patients at ini- tial dosages of 220-240 gg/day. In patient 5, a dosage of 400 gg/day was still effective 4 years later until his death from cancer, allowing him to move independently in his wheelchair and to stand and walk short distances with a little help. This contrasted with patient 6, in whom the in- trathecal baclofen had to be successively increased within the following 14 months up to the maximum tolerable dosage of 1440 gg/day. In no case was oral baclofen ef- fective alone, even in doses up to 150 mg/day. In two pa- tients it was successfully prescribed as a co-medication to benzodiazepines. The differen- tial diagnosis comprises a wide variety of diseases having in common stiff muscles or spasms, or both. Besides atyp- ical manifestations of, for example, polyneuropathy [38], myelitis [14], spondylotic myelopathy [39], or spinal tu- mours [49], some rare neuromuscular or central motor disturbances have to be taken into account: circumscribed or generalized muscle fibrosis [37], neuromyotonia [16], progressive cramping disease [41], startle disease [2], tetanus or strychnine poisoning, axial dystonia and PERM [51]. Particularly the last-mentioned disease shares some characteristic features with SMS, but is often associated with some of the signs listed among its exclusion criteria, and it typically follows a progressive course until death. The recent discovery of autoantibodies againgst GAD in about 60% of patients clinically diagnosed as having SMS [44] has distinctly improved the diagnostic accuracy. Our investigation of eight patients with SMS, four of six tested with GAD autoantibodies, revealed four new clini- cal features: 1. Spasmodic jerks, whether spontaneous or induced, show a chracteristic motor pattern with opisthotonos, stiff- ening of the legs or arms, or both, and inversion of the plantarflexed feet. This motor pattern is stereotyped, with a jerky onset and spasmodic lessening. However, the 163 movement itself is limited, apparently by co-contraction of antagonists. Spasmodic jerks are easily attenuated or even abolished by benzodiazepine treatment. 2. Spontaneous spasmodic jerks may be preceded by a particular feeling that an attack is imminent. As fear is known to induce spasmodic attacks [13, 33], such attacks might in fact be provoked by the fear of them. However, no fear was evident in patients 1 and 5 when expecting an attack, and the time-course and manifestation of this par- ticular feeling in a given patient were so uniform that some relationship to epileptic aura might be considered. 3. Intense paroxysmal fear is almost specifically asso- ciated with crossing a free space unaided, or even think- ing of it. This symptom must not be misunderstood as common agoraphobia as it may disappear instantaneously with minimum support. 4. The symptoms of patients tested positive or negative for GAD autoantibodies, respectively, do not differ. More- over autoimmunity does not discriminate between SMS and SMS "plus" cases. Two of our patients died unexpectedly, apparently in the course of vegetative crises. This demonstrates that such crises may be life-threatening [12] and underlines both the necessity for adequate symptomatic therapy and the hazards of provocation tests with, for example, clomipramine [48] or reserpine [28]. Acute autonomic failure has been suggested to cause sudden death in SMS, and has recently been suggested to result from autoim- mune-mediated hypothalamic disinhibition [32]. How- ever, we are not aware of any vegetative crisis without se- vere spontaneous spasms. This suggests that either the former are triggered by the latter or both are triggered by a common - hitherto unknown - mechanism. Site of the disturbance Neuroimaging abnormalities with variable appearance were found in only three of seven patients. White matter changes like those in patient 3 may occasionally be seen in normal subjects of similar age [11]. In patient 1, they might be considered as the remnants of a disorder of the white matter of unknown aetiology. Microscopical changes in the brain and spinal cord in patient 4, although obscured by changes due to dissoci- ated brain death, were suggestive of an inflammatory process. Indeed, this patient had had mildly elevated CSF cell counts on some occasions (Table 2). Perivascular lymphocyte cuffs and loss of neurons are prominent mi- croscopic features of PERM, but have been reported with a more scattered distribution in the brains and spinal cords of autopsied cases with SMS, too [32, 43]. So far, anatomical and neuroimaging studies have failed to provide evidence in favour of a cricumscribed le- sion or for a certain agent causing SMS. However, clues as to the tentative locus of the disturbance might be de- rived by analogy from cases with intense segmental rigid- ity and spasms or jerks in the corresponding muscles due to tumours of the spinal cord [36, 40, 43, 49], or spinal vascular lesions [8, 10]. In such cases, the most obvious microscopical finding was a distinct loss of small neurons (i.e. interneurons) in the respective spinal cord segments, accentuated in the inner parts of the anterior horns. This lesion pattern closely resembles the one reported for PERM and clinically related cases [5, 15, 18, 20, 50, 51], including one case with SMS [35]. The brunt of micro- scopical changes was found in the spinal cord, particu- larly in the ventromedial parts of its nuclear columns, sometimes associated with presumably secondary degen- eration of ascending tracts. In those autopsied cases, stiff- ness and spasms of jerks were the initial and most promi- nent clinical findings. This suggests that stiffness and spasms in SMS may also be of spinal origin, generated within the spinal grey matter. The particular involvement of axial muscles might reflect the location of their motor nuclei in the medial parts of the ventral horns, i.e. that re- gion which, on microscopical examination of clinically related cases, often showed the most severe loss of neu- rons and gliosis. Biochemical aspects The CSF has been postulated as normal in SMS [51]. However, Moersch and Woltman [33] reported a positive Nonne test in their patient 1, suspicious of intrathecal de novo IgG synthesis ([42, 47]; see also [22, 23, 45]). In our series, inconsistent CSF abnormalities were found (Table 2) varying from normal to suggestive of some mild in- flammation, even in one and the same patient on repeated examinations. Autoantibodies directed against GAD, the enzyme synthesizing GABA, were found in both serum and CSF in four of six patients tested, a finding that fits well with recently published data [44]. This high propor- tion raises the possibility that autoimmunity directed against GABAergic neurons may cause SMS [44]. Detection for autoantibodies against GAD seems to open up new perspectives for both the diagnosis and un- derstanding of SMS. Their heigh incidence as well as their high titres make them a potent diagnostic marker [46], irrespective of further clarification whether GAD au- toantibodies are a cause or a consequence of SMS [44]. At first sight autoimmunity directed against GABAergic neu- rons would explain the clinical symptoms of SMS with alluring plausibility as a manifestation of neuronal disin- hibition. However, GABA is probably the most wide- spread inhibitory transmitter within the central nervous system, and dense accumulations of GABAergic neurons or synapses, respectively, are found in many structures such as the neocortex, the cerebeallar cortex, the basal ganglia, or the hypothalamus [34]. An immunopathy di- rected against such ubiquitous neurons should lead to more physical signs than just stiffness and spasms. Possi- bly a differential susceptibility of certain subgroups of GABAergic neurons to immune-mediated disturbances, e.g. by expression of GAD isoforms (see [44]), might ac- count for some of these discrepancies. Another specula- tive explanation could be that the confusing variety of SMS "plus" cases (Table 4) might reflect wide distribu- tion of target neurons subjected to the postulated autoim- mune attack. It is noteworthy that two patients with GAD autoantibodies and the clinical or microscopical (or both) features of PERM - or SMS "plus" - have been published recently [4, 32 (case 2)], corresponding to our patient 6. The identification of autoantibodies directed against GABAergic neurons in both PERM and SMS, but so far not in other neurological diseases [44], supports the sug- gestion that PERM and SMS are closely related [18]. SMS possibly represents a benign and oligosymptomatic manifestation of a spectrum of encephalo-myelopathies having autoimmunity against GABAergic neurons in common.
