ARTHRITIS & RHEUMATISM Volume 37 Number 6, June 1994, pp 915-918 0 1994, American College of Rheumatology 915 TREATMENT OF STIFF-MAN SYNDROME WITH INTRAVENOUS IMMUNE GLOBULIN ELIZABETH W. KARLSON, LEWIS SUDARSKY, ERIC RUDERMAN, SUSAN PIERSON, MARY SCOTT, and SIMON M. HELFGOTT Objective. To evaluate the efficacy of intravenous immune globulin (IVIG) in the treatment of stiff-man syndrome (SMS). Methods. An open, unblinded study of 3 patients with active disease andor disease refractory to treat- ment with diazepam andor corticosteroids. Results. All 3 bedridden patients improved sub- stantially shortly after infusion with IVIG and regained function. Conclusion. IVIG may be useful for the treat- ment of SMS. Stiff-man syndrome (SMS) is an autoimmune neurologic disorder characterized by muscular stiff- ness in the trunk and limbs, with superimposed painful spasms (I). Sixty percent of patients have autoanti- bodies directed against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) (2), an enzyme present both in neurons secret- ing yaminobutyric acid (GABA) and in pancreatic beta cells. Organ-specific autoimmune diseases, in particular, insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus and hyper- thyroidism, are often seen in patients with SMS (3). We report herein, the use of high-dose intravenous immune globulin (IVIG) in the treatment of SMS refractory to other therapies (Table 1). CASE REPORTS Patient 1. This patient, a 50-year-old African- American woman, presented with a I-year history of pro- gressive stiffness of her lower extremities and difficulty walking. Previously, there had been transient improvement with a combination of physical therapy and diazepam. Several months earlier, treatment with prednisone, 60 mg/ day, and a course of 5 plasmapheresis treatments had resulted in a gain of 15" of flexion in both hips and the right knee. However, at the time of admission, she had persistent extensor tone of both lower extremities, with virtually no flexion of the hips, and she could not walk. There was hyperextension and spasm of both knees, and the ankles were maintained in constant plantar flexion. She was begun on a regimen of diazepam 5 mg 4 times a day and baclofen 20 mg 3 times a day. The electromyogram (EMG) showed continuous mo- tor unit activity in the lower extremities and normal findings in the upper extremities. Serum anti-GAD antibodies were detected by immunocytochemical assay. Treatment with diazepam, baclofen, carbamazepine, and valproic acid did not result in any improvement, nor did an intrathecal infu- sion of baclofen. Because of her lack of progress, treatment with IVIG was initiated, at a dosage of 400 mgkg/day for 5 consecutive days. Improvement in bilateral hip and right knee flexion was noted within a few days; IVIG therapy was repeated (400 mglkglday for 3 infusions) on a monthly basis. Follow- ing her third month of WIG therapy, hip flexion increased to 70" bilaterally, right knee flexion to 90", and left knee flexion to 10". She was able to walk with the use of a walker. The prednisone was gradually discontinued, and daily doses of baclofen and diazepam were reduced. Patient 2. This patient, a 43-year-old African- American woman, presented with a 3-year history of pro- gressive muscle stiffness, with spasms of the legs and lower back. This patient, a 69-year-old Caucasian woman, was seen because of her recent inability to walk. Six years earlier, a diagnosis of epilepsy had been established. Her seizures were focal and characterized by speech distur- bance and rigidity of the right lower extremity. She was maintained on a regimen of sodium valproate and diazepam. Four months prior to admission, she noted the onset of left lower extremity stiffness and difficulty walking. Physical examination revealed an extended left knee to 180" and extreme plantar flexion of the left ankle and foot. The patient could not walk. Results of MRI of the head and lumbar spine were unremarkable. An EMG of the lower extremities revealed continuous motor-unit activity. Anti- GAD antibodies were negative by immunocytochemical assay. The diazepam dosage was increased to 15 mg/day, with some clinical improvement noted; however, she be- came confused and disoriented as a result of this treatment. IVIG was begun (400 mg/kg/day for 5 days) and following the second infusion, the patient noted a marked resolution of left leg stiffness. She regained independent mobility for the next 5 weeks. Over the next 3 months, 2 additional courses of WIG therapy were administered after the patient became stiff and unable to walk. Each course resulted in a rapid return to full, independent ambulation. METHODS Immunocytochemistry. Immunorhodamine and immunoperoxidase staining of frozen tissue sections and primary cell cultures was carried out as previously described (2,3). Rat tissues were fixed by transcardiac perfusion. Small fragments of apparently normal human cerebral cortex were fixed by immersion. Patients' sera were used at dilutions of 1:20 to 1 :30 for immunofluorescence and at 1: 150 for immuno- peroxidase. Solimena et a1 (2,3) have identified anti-GAD antibodies in the majority of patients. Recently, those authors described autoantibodies to a 128-kd synaptic protein in 3 women with SMS and breast cancer who did not have anti-GAD antibodies (6). Grimaldi et a1 (7) have defined 3 diagnostic subsets of patients. The first group have autoimmune features, including insulin- dependent diabetes mellitus and thyroid disease, as well as the presence of multiple autoantibodies, such as anti-GAD, anti-islet cell, and other organic-specific antibodies. The second group of patients are anti-GAD antibody negative but have autoantibodies to other synaptic proteins, without the presence of organ- specific autoantibodies. These patients have devel- oped malignancies, including breast carcinoma, colon carcinoma, and Hodgkin’s lymphoma. The third group of SMS patients do not display any autoimmune features clinically and are anti-GAD antibody nega- tive. Patients 1 and 2 belong to the first group, and patient 3 to the third group. While SMS is considered to be an autoimmune disorder, the pathophysiology is poorly understood. There is a dominant autoimmune response directed against GAD in a high proportion of patients, with intrathecal production of the autoantibody (2,3). GAD is an intracellular enzyme involved in the synthesis of the neurotransmitter GABA in the central nervous system. GAD is specifically present within GABA neurons, but is not expressed as a cell surface antigen. The rigidity and spasm seen clinically reflect a prepon- derance of excitatory over inhibitory influences on the motor neuron pool. A deficiency of GABA-mediated inhibition at the spinal and/or supraspinal level has been proposed (3). Solimena et al(2) found that serum from affected patients reacts with nerve terminals of cerebellar basket cells in the rat. In human SMS, it is unclear whether symptoms are a direct result of dam- age to GABA-synthetic enzymes or some other neu- ronal target. The rapid response to IVIG in our 3 patients suggests that the autoantibodies might have some direct action on GABA release or a blocking effect at an ion exchange channel or synaptic receptor. Intravenous immune globulin has been used in the treatment of such neurologic autoimmune diseases as myasthenia gravis (8,9), multiple sclerosis (lo), Guillain-Barre syndrome (1 l), demyelinating poly- neuropathy (12), and polymyositis (13). Given the refractory nature of our patients’ clinical conditions, we elected to treat with IVIG. The striking and rapid onset of clinical improvement in our 3 patients may suggest that IVIG is effective via an antiidiotypic antibody mechanism. Antiidiotypic antibodies capable of binding to the idiotypic region of a specific disease- associated autoantibody can be found in IVIG and in the serum of patients recovering from a disease asso- ciated with that autoantibody, but they cannot be found in the serum of healthy subjects (14). Alterna- tively, receptors for the Fc portion of IgG on reticu- loendothelial cells may be blocked by IVIG impairing their ability to function (8). The neurologic improvement in our patients following peripheral administration of IVIG suggests that immune globulin administered intravenously af- fects the production and action of autoantibodies in the intrathecal compartment and central nervous sys- tem. In theory, the blood-brain barrier excludes exog- enously administered IgG, though Sekul et a1 (15) demonstrated that IgG is elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid following peripheral intravenous administration. Due to the nature of the immunocytochemical assay used, we could not quantify a change in the titer of the anti-GAD antibodies before and after treatment with IVIG. The natural course of SMS is one of steady, slow progression of the disease (16). Spontaneous remissions are extremely unusual and would be an unlikely explanation for our patients’ clinical improve- ment. Although a delayed benefit from corticosteroid therapy may occur, both corticosteroid-treated pa- tients had been receiving at least 60 mg prednisone per day for several weeks without benefit prior to treat- ment with IVIG. Prior to initiation of treatment with IVIG, all 3 patients were treated with maximally tolerated doses of diazepam but remained bedridden. Following IVIG therapy the doses of concomitant drugs, including corticosteroids, diazepam, and ba- clofen, could be reduced in all 3 patients. We suggest that further studies are essential to verify our initial observations of clinical benefit with the use of IVIG in SMS.
