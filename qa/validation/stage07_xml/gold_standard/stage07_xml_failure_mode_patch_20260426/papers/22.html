Hunt, and Stanley Fahn Department of Neurology. The Neurological Institute, Columbia-Presbyterian Medical Center, New York, New York, U.S.A. Summary: We performed a therapeutic trial with the glycine precursor, mila- cemide, on 10 patients with intractable movement disorders. Six had myoclo- nus of various etiologies and one each had progressive supranuclear palsy, Filipino X-linked dystonia with parkinsonism, painful legs and moving toes, and stiff-person syndrome. Milacemide was initiated at a dose of 2,400 mg/day, orally, and increased gradually to a maximum of 4,800 mg/day. No clear-cut observable improvement occurred. There were no serious adverse effects. Myoclonus refers to sudden, brief, shock-like in- voluntary movements caused by active muscle con- tractions (positive myoclonus) or by sudden, brief lapses of tonic contraction in active postural mus- cles (negative myoclonus) (1). Current therapy for myoclonus is often not satisfactory. Treatments may include clonazepam, valproic acid, primidone, and L-5-hydroxytryptophan (L-5-HTP) with carbi- dopa (to prevent peripheral decarboxylation). In ad- dition, piracetam, fluoxetine, clomipramine, dopa- mine or dopamine agonists, opiates (codeine), tri- hexyphenidyl, carbamazepine, and tetrabenazine have occasionally been beneficial. Specific thera- pies for the various types of myoclonus have not been delineated. Epileptic and cortical myoclonus may respond best to anticonvulsants, such as clo- nazepam, valproic acid, and primidone. The brainstem and spinal cord inhibitory neuro- transmitter, glycine, has been implicated in the pathophysiology of myoclonus. The glycine receptor is involved in urea-induced myoclonus (3). Whole-brain and brainstem glycine levels are decreased in DDT-induced myoclonus in mice and rats (43). Intraventricular glycine sup- presses DDT-induced myoclonus (6). Animal models of myoclonus, implicating glycin- ergic dysfunction led Truong and Fahn (7) to try glycine in patients with myoclonus; however, gly- cine in doses up to 6 g/day was unsuccessful in the treatment of myoclonus. CSF glycine levels did not change during treatment; this finding suggested that this dose did not effectively enter the brain. Animal studies had previously shown that glycine does not easily cross the blood-brain barrier (8,9). Milacemide (2-n-pentylaminoacetamide), rnanu- factured by Searle, is a prodrug of glycine. Milace- mide readily penetrates the blood-brain barrier; in the brain; it is metabolized by monoamine oxi- dase-B (MAO-B) to glycineamide and then to gly- cine (10). In rats, systemic administration of mila- cemide blocks the myoclonus induced by DDT (1 1) as effectively as glycine infused into the ventricles (6). Glycine levels in the brain increase with admin- istration of milacemide, but serine levels do not change. Therefore, increasing brain glycine levels, 484 MILACEMIDE IN MYOCLONUS 485 either by systemic administration of milacemide or by intraventricular infusion of glycine, may be a mechanism to produce an antimyoclonic effect. It is not clear if milacemide, while preventing myoclo- nus in the DDT model, could also do so via seroto- nin neurotransmission since it inhibits MAO-B ac- tivity (12-14). Milacemide also increases y-ami- nobutyric acid (GABA) concentrations in the substantia nigra (10). Milacemide has an acceptable safety profile in animals (10). In humans, milacemide has been eval- uated for safety in healthy volunteers. Milacemide has been relatively well-tolerated in therapeutic tri- als conducted in patients with epilepsy (15). Ad- verse effects may include vasomotor disorders (flushing), nausea and vomiting, palpitations, and elevations in hepatic transaminases (10). These ab- normalities generally return to normal following cessation of drug or reduction of dosage (10). Since glycine, GABA, and the monoamines are major inhibitory neurotransmitters in the CNS, mi- lacemide may be a potential agent in the therapy of myoclonus and other movement disorders. Brown et al. (16), in an open-label trial of milacemide ti- trated to 2,400 mglday in seven subjects with my- oclonus and three with stiff-person syndrome, found no significant improvement. We report a trial of milacemide in nine patients with intractable movement disorders. METHODS Milacemide was obtained from Searle (Skokie, IL, U.S.A.). The study was approved by the Insti- tutional Review Board, and written informed con- sent was obtained from each subject enrolled. The study had three phases. Phase I was an open- label dose-titration lead-in, beginning at a dosage of 800 mg, orally, three times a day, and not exceeding 1,600 mg three times a day (4,800 mglday). During Phase I, patients who did not respond or who ex- perienced significant treatment-related adverse events were withdrawn. Phase I1 was a double- blind, randomized design that employed the optimal dose determined in Phase I or a matching placebo at the same dose. Subjects were transferred immedi- ately from Phase I to Phase 11, without gradual withdrawal of drug. Phase 111 was similar to Phase 11, without gradual withdrawal of drug. Phase 111 was similar to Phase 11, with the blinded treatment reversed. The study was terminated when the max- imal duration of treatment in Phase I11 was attained. Subjects could participate as inpatients or outpa- tients. In Phase I, dose titration occurred over 12 days for inpatients and over 21 days for outpatients, followed by 1 week of treatment at the optimal dose. In Phase I1 and Phase 111, inpatients received milacemide or placebo for 1 week and outpatients received milacemide or placebo for 3 weeks. Potential subjects were men or nonpregnant women (either sterile or using adequate contracep- tives) between the ages of 18 and 90 years who suf- fered from myoclonus or another intractable move- ment disorder and who were otherwise in good health. For at least 30 days before enrollment, sub- jects were on a stable regimen of any drugs used to treat their myoclonus or other movement disorder. Patients with serious medical conditions or labora- tory abnormalities, recent histories of alcohol or drug abuse, other concurrent or recent investiga- tional drug studies, known hypersensitivity to mi- lacemide, or previous participation in this study were excluded. Screening was performed within 14 days of en- rollment and included informed consent, medical history, physical and neurologic examination, elec- trocardiogram, laboratory tests (complete blood count, electrolytes, liver functions, urine analysis, and pregnancy test), videotape, and assessment of severity. Global assessment was on a four-point scale: 0, no signs; 1, mild signs, not interfering with activities; 2, moderate signs, interfering with some activities; 3, severe signs, interfering with many ac- tivities; and 4, marked signs, interfering with most activities. Ratings were done by the subjects, family members, and treating physicians. Laboratories tests were repeated during the study and obtained at the end of each phase. Ten subjects were enrolled: six with myoclonus of various etiologies and four others with intracta- ble movement disorders (one each with progressive supranuclear palsy, Filipino x-linked dystonia with parkinsonism, painful legs and moving toes, and stiff-person syndrome; Table 1). Movement Disorders, Vul. 8, No. 4, 1993 MILACEMIDE IN MYOCLONUS 487 RESULTS The clinical rating score did not change signifi- cantly in nine patients treated with milacemide. Only one (case 6, with presumed cortical-basal gan- glionic degeneration) had some apparent benefit with less severe myoclonus in the open-label trial. He continued through Phase I1 and Phase I11 with- out clear benefit. Eight of the I0 subjects completed Phase I (the open trial) to a maximum dose of 4,800 mglday. Two patients withdrew from the study due to side effects at 3,600 mglday without any benefit. A pa- tient (case 3) with rhythmic myoclonus experienced excessive appetite. The patient (case 8) with painful legs and moving toes had a transient exacerbation of her condition. Both side effects resolved after discontinuation of milacemide. Full blood counts, electrolytes, and liver function tests did not change significantly. DISCUSSION The 10 subjects in our study did not benefit from milacemide in doses up to 4,800 mg/day. Brown et al. (16), in an open-label trial of milacemide titrated to 2,400 mg/day in seven subjects with myoclonus and three with stiff-man syndrome, also found no significant improvement. The inhibitory neurotransmitter, glycine, was un- successful in the treatment of myoclonus in doses up to 6 g/day (7), possibly due to poor penetration of glycine into the brain from the peripheral circula- tion. However, milacemide, a glycine precursor known to readily cross the blood-brain barrier, did not improve the myoclonus in the six myoclonus patients in this study or in the seven patients pre- viously studied (16). It is possible that the dose of milacemide was too low to sufficiently increase gly- cine levels in the CNS. However, the ineffective- ness of milacemide in these cases of myoclonus may suggest that defective glycinergic neurotrans- mission is not an important factor in myoclonus. Precise mechanisms for the various types of myoc- lonus are not known. Posthypoxic myoclonus may involve serotonergic (21,22) and GABA-ergic (23) neurotransmission, as suggested by reduced CSF levels of 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (SHIAA), the major metabolite of serotonin, and of GABA. Post- anoxic myoclonus improves with drugs that en- hance serotonin activity [such as L-~-HTP, the im- mediate synthetic precursor to serotonin; tryp- tophan with a monoamine oxidase inhibitor (22); or fluoxetine, an inhibitor of serotonin reuptake (24)] and also with drugs, such as valproic acid and clo- nazepam, that enhance GABA-ergic inhibitory in- fluence (21,25). Brown et al. (16) postulated that the discrepancy between the effectiveness of glycine and milace- mide in improving DDT-induced myoclonus and their ineffectiveness in treating myoclonus in hu- mans may be due to the fact that animal experi- ments for myoclonus involve manipulation of the reticular formation and thus may not be an appro- priate model for cortical and spinal myoclonus. However, our case 2 is believed to have reticular reflex myoclonus (18), and she failed to improve. Defective glycinergic systems have been impli- cated in some animal models of spinal rigidity (26,27). Schmidt et al. (28) noted that the rigidity in stiff-person syndrome resembles that produced by strychnine poisoning and experimental cord isch- emia, both of which are associated with a loss of normal glycinergic inhibition to motoneurons from spinal interneurons. This observation provoked speculation that abnormalities in glycinergic inhibi- tion by spinal interneurons might play a role in stiff- person syndrome (28). Trials of glycine in stiff- person syndrome (28,29) were not successful, possibly because of the poor penetration of the blood-brain barrier by glycine (7). There is more evidence for an imbalance in the GABA-ergic system in stiff-person syndrome. Med- ications with GABA-ergic properties, such as diaz- epam (30), valproic acid (31), clonazepam (32), and baclofen (33,34), are beneficial in some patients. Antibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase, the enzyme that synthesizes GABA, are present in the serum and CSF of many stiff-person patients (35,36). We offer two possible explanations for the lack of benefit in subjects treated with milacemide. Milace- mide and its metabolite, glycine, may have no role in the treatment of myoclonus, stiff-person syn- drome, or the other diseases studied, or the dose may have been insufficient to increase glycine lev- els in the brain.
