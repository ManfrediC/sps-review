Movement Disorders Vol. 6, No. 1, 1991, pp. 12-20 0 1991 Movement Disorder Society Stiff-Person Syndrome: An Autoimmune Disease Philip Blum and Joseph Jankovic Department of Neurology, Baylor College of Medicine, Houston, Texas, U.S.A. Summary: Stiff-person syndrome (SPS) is characterized by progressive, usu- ally symmetric rigidity of the axial muscles with superimposed painful spasms precipitated by tactile stimuli, passive stretch, volitional movement of affected or unaffected muscles, startling noises, and emotional stimuli. Electromyog- raphy demonstrates continuous normal motor unit potentials in the affected muscles. Both the rigidity and the spasms are relieved by sleep, general anes- thesia, myoneural blockade, peripheral nerve blockade, and partially by diaz- epam. Evidence for an autoimmune etiology of SPS includes its association with other autoimmune diseases and autoantibodies and the presence of anti- bodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of many affected patients. We describe two patients with this syndrome who had GAD antibodies in both CSF and serum. Partial relief of the symp- toms in these patients by corticosteroid therapy provides additional evidence of an autoimmune etiology of SPS and of the role of immunotherapy in its treatment. Key Words: Stiff-person syndrome-Autoimmune diseases-corti- costeroid drugs. Stiff-man syndrome was first described by Moersch and Woltman (1) in a series of 14 cases in 1956. The world’s literature was critically reviewed and diagnostic criteria were proposed by Gordon et al. (2) in 1967. Because the disease affects both men and women, we prefer the term “stiff-person’’ syn- drome (SPS). Patients with SPS have progressive, usually symmetric.rigidity of the axial muscles, and painful spasms that may fluctuate in intensity. Spasms may be precipitated by tactile stimuli, pas- sive stretch, volitional movement of affected or un- affected muscles, startling noises, and emotional stimuli. There is often tenderness and palpable ri- gidity of the axial and proximal limb musculature. Deep tendon reflexes are occasionally increased, but may be decreased. Both the rigidity and the spasms are at least partially relieved by sleep, general an- esthesia, myoneural blockade, peripheral nerve blockade, and usually, diazepam. The improvement in the cardinal symptoms is accompanied by cessa- tion of the continuous motor unit potentials. Cranial nerve involvement, respiratory compromise, and deforming skeletal sequelae are common. Myoclo- nus, seizures, and psychiatric illnesses have also been reported in some patients. Treatment of SPS has changed little since Howard (3) introduced diazepam as the drug of choice in 1963. However, in 1988 Solimena et al. (4) provided the first solid evidence of an autoimmune mecha- nism of the disease and introduced hope for new effective therapies. These investigators demon- strated the presence of autoantibodies against glu- tamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) in both the serum and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of their patient with the syndrome. In their follow-up report, Soli- mena et al. (5) observed GAD autoantibodies in 20 STIFF-PERSON SYNDROME 13 of 33 patients with the syndrome. We describe here two patients with SPS found to have GAD autoanti- bodies in their sera and CSF, and whose associated autoimmune diseases, other autoantibodies, and re- sponse to corticosteroid drugs provide additional evidence of an autoimmune etiology and a role for immunotherapy in this disabling disease. PATIENT 1 A 63-year-old Hispanic woman was referred for evaluation of increased tone in her lower extremi- ties. She had been well until 6 years previously, when she began to experience occasional episodes of sudden, painful stiffening of the left hip and knee, usually provoked by walking. The spasms lasted 10-15 s, and then resolved spontaneously. Her symptoms progressed, increasing in severity and frequency, and in June 1988 the patient's left leg became persistently stiff, rendering her unable to walk. At that time, she also had severe, chronic, recurrent back and abdominal pain. By the end of the year, her right leg had also begun to stiffen. The stiffness was much decreased during sleep. There was no history of diabetes, trauma, seizures, myo- clonus, weakness, or sensory difficulties. Her or- thopedist had prescribed multiple medications including propoxyphene napsylate and acetamino- phen (Darvocet), quinine sulfate, phenytoin, so- dium dantrolene, carbamazepine, baclofen, and di- azepam. Only the last two provided at least partial relief of her spasms. Past medical history was sig- nificant only for a cholecystectomy in 1976 and treated pulmonary tuberculosis with documented recovery. She has not used alcohol or tobacco prod- ucts. No one else in her family has had symptoms similar to hers. Both of her sisters died of compli- cations of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM). Review of systems was significant for long-standing vitiligo. Physical examination on admission to our service revealed a small, chronically ill-appearing woman lying in bed. She had vitiligo of the hands, feet, and neck. There was increased tone and tenderness in the muscles of the neck. Her paraspinous muscles were markedly rigid and tender and she had severe kyphoscoliosis. Tone and strength in the arms was normal. However, tone was markedly increased in her legs, with palpable rigidity and tenderness of the muscles. Her feet were held in the equinus position and her knees were hyperextended by her stiffness (videotape segment 1). There were no fascicula- tions. Strength could not be assessed because of the rigidity. Plantar response was silent, and deep ten- don reflexes could not be elicited in the knees or ankles. Functionally, the patient was unable to walk because of the stiffness in her legs and was unable to sit up because of the deformity of her spine. She had an intact intellect and sensorium and there were no other neurologic abnormalities. Results of routine laboratory studies including se- rum electrolytes, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), cre- atinine, glucose, and liver enzymes were normal; creatinine phosphokinase was 256. Lyme titers were negative. Radiographs of the chest and lum- bosacral spine demonstrated arthritic changes only. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy, ultrasound of the abdomen, and computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen all were normal. Lumbar myelogram, CT of the brain, and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging of the lumbosacral spine were also normal. Muscle biopsy revealed mild neurogenic atrophy and type I1 atrophy. A complete myelogram revealed only mild cervical spondylosis with neither stenosis of the canal nor cord compression. Conduction studies of the right posterior tibial nerve were normal at 52 m/s, whereas that of the left posterior tibial nerve was slightly slowed at 44 mls. EMG demonstrated continuous spontaneous motor unit activity in all tested muscles of the left leg and in the lumbar and thoracic paraspinous muscles up to the midthoracic level bilaterally. Intermittent, spontaneous motor unit activity also occurred in the right leg and could be provoked by startling or tactile stimulation. There were no fasciculations, fibrillations, or posi- tive sharp waves. Spinal epidural blockade was performed with tet- racaine (Pontocaine) at the T9 level. The patient's pain was relieved, and her rigidity decreased enough to allow 20" of active flexion in the left knee and 40" in the right (videotape segment 2). EMG recording demonstrated cessation of the continuous motor activity within 10 min. Routine studies of the CSF taken at the time of the myelogram including glucose, protein, and cell count were all normal and no oligoclonal bands were detected. GAD autoanti- bodies, assayed by Dr. Solimena and Dr. De Camilli (5), were present in both the serum and CSF by light-microscopy immunocytochemistry and West- ern blot. In addition, autoantibodies to islet cells and gastric parietal cells were present in the serum. This patient was reported as case 17 in the second report of Solimena et al. (5). The patient was unable to tolerate diazepam in high doses because of excessive sedation. The com- Movement Disorders, Vol. 6, No. I, 1991 14 P. BLUM AND J. JANKOVIC bination of 50 mg of baclofen and 30 mg of diazepam daily provided only modest relief of symptoms. One hundred milligrams of prednisone per day was added to this regimen. Within 10 days, the patient noted a marked decrease in her stiffness and spasm. One year later, baclofen has been discontinued, di- azepam has been decreased to 15 mg per day, and prednisone is slowly being tapered down from 40 mg every other day. The stiffness in her hips, knees, and back is markedly reduced. Residual stiffness holds her ankles in a position of plantar flexion bilaterally. She is now capable of sitting without aid, although she cannot walk because of her ankle contractures. PATIENT 2 A 53-year-old black man was referred for evalu- ation of a 2?h-year history of progressive stiffness of the lower extremities. His initial symptom was in- voluntary upward curling of the toes of both feet. Stiffness subsequently developed in the remaining muscles of his legs. At the same time, he began to have chronic neck and low-back pain. One year be- fore admission, the patient also began to have pain- ful spasms of his legs superimposed on his stiffness, usually provoked by passive stretching. His wife noted that his spasms ceased and his stiffness re- solved somewhat during sleep. He began to require assistance arising from bed and walking. There was no history of diabetes, trauma, seizures, myoclo- nus, weakness, or sensory symptoms. The patient had been treated unsuccessfully with nonsteroidal antiinflammatory medications, sodium dantrolene, carbamazepine, trihexyphenidyl, and carbidopa/ levodopa (Sinemet). Treatment with phenytoin af- forded him mild relief. Low-dose diazepam moder- ately improved his stiffness and he was able to walk without assistance. One of the patient’s sisters has IDDM; otherwise, the family history was unre- markable. General physical and neurological examination results were normal except for the motor system. Tone and strength were normal in the arms, but tone was markedly increased in all muscle groups of the legs, with dystonic posturing of both feet man- ifesting as extension of the metatarsal-phalangeal joints and flexion of the distal interphalangeal joints. Strength of the legs was difficult to assess because of rigidity. Deep tendon reflexes were nor- mal throughout and plantar reflexes were silent. The patient walked stiffly and turned with great dif- ficulty. The following laboratory test results were nor- mal: electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, glucose, uric acid, calcium, phosphorus, total protein, albumin, serum electrophoresis, liver enzymes, folate, vita- min B 12, hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, platelet count, urinalysis, erythrocyte sed- imentation rate, rapid plasma reagin, antinuclear antibody, and rheumatoid factor. A 5-h glucose tol- erance test also showed normal results. A complete myelogram revealed mild lumbar and cervical spondylosis without stenosis of the canal. Radio- graphs of the chest and spine, electrocardiogram, electroencephalogram, and somatosensory evoked potentials were all unremarkable. Muscle biopsy was similar to that of patient 1. MR imaging of the brain and spinal cord were normal. EMG revealed the presence of continuous spontaneous motor unit potentials in the muscles of the legs, which was abolished by spinal epidural block. As the block began to wear off, the patient was able to move his legs nearly normally. CSF glucose, protein, and cell count were unremarkable and there were no oligo- clonal bands. CSF IgG/albumin ratio was normal. Autoantibodies to GAD were detected in both se- rum and CSF by the method of Solimena et al. (4). In addition, gastric parietal cell, pancreatic islet cell, microsomal, and thyroglobulin autoantibodies were all present in the patient’s serum. This patient was reported as case 20 in the second report of Solimena et al. (5). The human lymphocyte antigen (HLA) phenotype of the patient was A3,24; B14(w6),w57(w4); DRw6,7(DQw1 ,w3; DRw52,53). At the time of admission, diazepam therapy was withdrawn slowly to determine the baseline muscle stiffness of the patient. After a few days, he became markedly stiffer (videotape segment 1). The patient then began supplemental treatment with 100 mg of prednisone per day for 1 week and then 100 and 20 mg on an alternate-day basis. Within 10 days of starting prednisone, the patient began to notice a marked decrease of his stiffness (videotape segment 2). He was able to decrease the dose of diazepam from 40 to 20 mg per day. A year later, his dose of prednisone has been decreased to 40 mg every other day, but he still requires 20 mg of diazepam daily for residual stiffness. DISCUSSION Our two patients demonstrate the typical findings of SPS as described by Moersch and Woltman (1). Both fulfill the diagnostic criteria of Gordon et al. (2). The stiffness as well as the EMG findings improved after spinal anesthesia. In addition, both of our patients exhibited evi- dence of autoimmunity. The abolition of symptoms and of the characteristic EMG find- ings by peripheral nerve and motor endplate block- ing agents, sleep, and general and spinal anesthesia provide evidence for a CNS origin of the syndrome. Gordon et al. (2) suggested overactivity of the y motor neuron system or underactivity of their su- prasegmental inhibitory influences as the cause of the excessive discharges in the M motor neuron pool. Subsequent electrophysiologic and pharmaco- logic studies have provided evidence implicating dysfunction of GABAergic neurons in the y motor neuron system as the mechanism of the continuous muscle activity (13,17,26,36). Clinical improvement with diazepam, which is known to potentiate GABAergic transmission (13), also suggests in- volvement of the CNS in the pathophysiology of the syndrome. More recent investigation of the pathogenesis of SPS has been directed toward understanding the cause of the presumed y motor system dysfunction. In their first report of a patient with SPS, epilepsy, type I diabetes, and elevated IgG/albumin ratio in the CSF, Solimena et al. (4) recovered antibody di- rected against GABAergic neurons. This antibody, recovered from the serum and CSF, demonstrated immunoreactivity by light-microscopy immunocy- tochemistry in a pattern identical to the cellular and subcellular distributions of GAD in rat and human brain and rat pancreatic islet cells. In addition, this antibody was found to recognize essentially one Movement Disorders, Vol. 6, No. I. 1991 18 P. BLUM AND J. JANKOVIC band comigrating with GAD on Western blot (4). In their subsequent study, they found these GAD au- toantibodies in 20 of 33 patients with SPS and again noted close association with a variety of other au- toimmune diseases, particularly IDDM (5). Because GAD is primarily an intracellular antigen, autoanti- bodies directed against this enzyme may represent a phenomenon secondary to the immunologic de- struction of GABAergic neurons. These observa- tions strongly support an autoimmune hypothesis for SPS. An autoimmune mechanism of disease was first proposed for SPS by Young (7) in 1966, because, in addition to motor complaints, his patient had per- nicious anemia and possible Hashimoto’s thyroid- itis. Autoimmune diseases often coexist in patients. For example, the occurrence of pernicious anemia is substantially increased in patients with idiopathic adrenal insufficiency, Grave’s disease, and vitiligo (37). Similarly, a striking number of autoimmune diseases occur in patients with SPS (Table 1). Of the 84 patients who fulfill Gordon’s criteria (2) for SPS, 15, or 18%, had clinical evidence of one or more autoimmune diseases. Three had pernicious ane- mia, four had vitiligo, and one patient developed myasthenia gravis after a spontaneous remission of stiff-person symptoms. Four of the 84 had hypothy- roidism and 2 had hyperthyroidism; both of these diseases may be autoimmune in origin. In addition, two patients were thought to have Hashimoto’s thy- roiditis. Our patient 1 had vitiligo as well as two sisters with IDDM. Patient 2 also had a sister with IDDM. Autoantibodies are also commonly found in the sera of patients with SPS (Table 2). In the sec- ond report of Solimena et al. (9, 19 of 20 patients with SPS and GAD autoantibody present in CSF or serum also had at least one other type of autoanti- body. Of those who did not have GAD antibody, 4 of 12 had at least one other serum autoantibody present. Both patients described in this report had serum autoantibodies directed against islet cells and gastric parietal cells. Patient 2 also had microsomal and thyroglobulin antibodies. The remarkable num- ber of autoantibodies and autoimmune diseases in affected patients again suggests that SPS has an au- toimmune etiology. The relationship of SPS and diabetes mellitus de- serves special attention. The current hypothesis about the pathogenesis of IDDM is that an abnor- mal immune response to an unknown stimulus, pos- sibly a virus, is responsible for the development of IDDM in genetically predisposed individuals. Ninety-five percent of white patients with IDDM have HLA DR4 and/or DR3, suggesting that the disease is mediated by regulatory or structural vari- ation of HLA molecules (38). Review of the litera- ture reveals that 7 of the 84 (8%) patients were re- ported to have IDDM, a frequency more than 30 times that of the general population (0.25%) (39). Thirteen additional patients were reported to have DM but the type was not reported. Another two patients had islet cell antibodies (ICA) without clinical evidence of DM. In one study, patients with ICA who had relatives with IDDM had a 100% probability of de- veloping IDDM within 5 years (38). Thus, the cal- culated 30-fold increase in the frequency of IDDM in patients with SPS is a conservative estimate. The relationship between IDDM and SPS may not be coincidental. The peculiar configurations of HLA-determined immunoregulatory molecules im- plicated in the pathogenesis of IDDM (39) may rep- resent the altered immune system via which autoim- munity to GABAergic cells in the CNS develops. The demonstration of HLA phenotypes common to both diseases supports this hypothesis. HLA DR3 and DR4, the alleles most commonly associated with IDDM in whites, were present in all three pa- tients with SPS in whom this antigen was typed, including one who did not have IDDM (Table 2). In addition, four of five patients of Williams et al. (29) had HLA B44, which is in linkage dysequilibrium with DR4 (40). Typing of HLA-DR was not done in these patients. Our patient 2 had neither HLA DR3 nor DR4. However, this patient is black and the HLA phenotypes responsible for susceptibility to IDDM in populations other than whites are not well established (38). Nevertheless, specific HLA types may represent the etiologic link between IDDM and SPS . Improvement of SPS symptoms with immuno- therapy provides additional strong support for the autoimmune hypothesis of the disease as well as hope for curative treatment. Plasmapheresis has had a variable effect. Vicari et al. (28) reported striking improvement in their patient after 10 days of every-other-day plasma exchange. Harding et al. (30) reported one patient who had no improvement after 5 days of plasma exchange and another who failed to improve after combined treatment with plasmapheresis, azathioprine, and prednisone. She and her colleagues point out, however, that because GAD autoantibody synthesis seems to occur in- trathecally, exchange of plasma would theoretically not be expected to benefit these patients. Movement Disorders, Vol. 6, No. I, 1991 STIFF-PERSON SYNDROME 19 Corticosteroid immunotherapy has been more successful. Piccolo (28) has reported two cases in which patients responded dramatically within 10 days of steroid treatment. One patient treated with prednisone 100 mg/day achieved a complete remis- sion. The second also responded to prednisone 100 mg/day, but has had some residual contractures of affected muscles. Our two patients similarly had markedly reduced stiffness with improvement in their functioning and substantial reductions in their diazepam requirements. Residual contractures re- sulting from the prolonged muscle rigidity remain, however, and impair the function of both of our patients. Early intervention might be expected to produce more complete relief. In summary, two new cases of SPS are presented and the features of the disease are documented by videotape. Furthermore, we documented a marked decrease in stiffness and spasms in both patients after administration of corticosteroid drugs. They represent two of only four similarly treated patients with functional improvement after steroid therapy. The effectiveness of immunotherapy in these pa- tients adds to a growing body of evidence to support an autoimmune etiology of SPS.
